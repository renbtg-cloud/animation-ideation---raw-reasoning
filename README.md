*RAW REASONING: spontaneous scratches* is a proposed animated audiovisual work of approximately 100 minutes, conceived as a nonlinear opera made from narrative music videos, dramatic passages, interludes, recitative-like scenes, visual rhymes, and silence. It can shift from deliberately crude stick figures and profane family comedy to psychologically intimate performances and a single sequence of near-obsessive visual refinement.

This dossier presents **the story and the creative challenges**—not a finished screenplay or a claim that production has begun. The characters, scenes, and musical juxtapositions are intentional; some connecting scenes and the final chronology remain under development.

## **DOING** — Consolidated character, opening, and parallel-strand decisions (6 November 2026)

**Editorial frame.** *RAW REASONING* is **original fiction** shaped by cultural references, lived observations, and stories heard over time. No character or dramatic event in this dossier is to be presented as the biography of a named private individual, as a declaration addressed to an identifiable real person, or as a claim about the actual descendants of a historical figure. Family relationships described below are **relationships among fictional characters**. The audience is trusted to infer meanings without explanatory medical, moral, sociological, or religious lectures.

### **DOING** — Anne Marie François: origins, intellectual independence, spirituality

**Name and origin.** **Anne Marie François** is from **French Guiana**, *not Paramaribo, Suriname*. The formerly used **Grothendiek** is a fictional Grothendieck-related name or identity, whose exact use alongside François remains to be established; do not silently treat it as her birth surname. Her fatherhood connection with the historical Alexander Grothendieck is a **deliberate fictional invention, not historical biography**. The city within French Guiana, the full chronology of the couple's first meeting, and any older Paramaribo-specific reveal require reconciliation. **Separate scenes explicitly set in Paramaribo for other plot purposes are not automatically relocated.**

Anne Marie was a precocious mathematical prodigy already studying mathematics at university in French Guiana. She leaves the academic path and her oppressive, intellectually exploitative grandmother. Her departure is not a rejection of mathematics: it is also an indictment, from her perspective, of self-proclaimed wisdom, academic hierarchy and claims to moral authority. Her precise reasons may have accumulated over time rather than forming one fully articulated doctrine when she left. **The mathematical book she reveres is not for sale** even though she sells other books.

She is a **Rastafarian with a deeply lived Israelite/biblical religious self-understanding**. Her bodily worship and study are **real religious activity for the character**, never an empty imitation of Judaism or an exotic visual accessory. A slow, mournful, rocking devotion evokes prayer and study at the Western Wall; her dreadlocks can produce an intentional visual echo of the sidecurls worn by some Orthodox Jews. These are purposeful correspondences in the direction of the scene, not a pronouncement that all Rastas and Jews share an identical religious classification or practice. The precise camera direction should respect the sincerity of her devotion.

**Personal temperament.** Anne Marie speaks many languages, often comfortably accommodating visitors from very different countries in their own languages. When intellectually excited, she speaks extraordinarily fast, but can **deliberately slow down** and adjust to her listener. Her capabilities are demonstrated through interaction, not narrated as a résumé.

### **DOING** — The actual opening: station, two songs, self-forgiveness, and an ordinary workday

**This is the film's first scene.** The Camille–Gérard–Daygorô *Ratamahatta* material remains part of the work **but no longer opens it**.

At a busy railway station, Anne Marie sells books and intricate mathematical handicrafts; among the books for sale is Gabriel García Márquez's *The Incredible and Sad Tale of Innocent Eréndira and Her Heartless Grandmother*. Her own precious Grothendieck book is **not for sale**. She is not merely reading it: her relationship to the text joins mathematics, mourning, filial memory, and **religious ritual**. In visual and thematic terms the volume can function for her almost like a **private Torah-like text**—mathematical, paternal, repeatedly revisited, argued with, and capable of yielding new meaning as the reader changes—without equating mathematics with Judaism and without any character naming or explaining that analogy on screen.

**Musical movement I — Príncipe Messias.** The scene starts in the sorrowful register of **“Papéis e Papéis”** by **Príncipe Messias**. Its thematic tensions include self-proclaimed sages, poets of the street, books and thought, and the struggle to oppose terror with courage and love. At the passage referring to poets of the street crying over the book of thoughts, Anne Marie moves **slowly, sorrowfully and ritually**, in an oscillation recalling prayer and the bodily expressiveness of **Bob Marley**, but initially weighed down by grief. She imagines her father **Alexander Grothendieck** dancing slowly and sadly to the music, and grieves intensely for his suffering. **His ashes are part of the scene's memorial significance**, but *where they are kept* has not been specified and must not be invented. The movement is a sincere spiritual act and an intimate lament, not decorative choreography.

**Musical movement II — the reason, the light, and self-forgiveness.** In a paragraph or page of Grothendieck she discovers **a reason**: the idea has mathematical force and also opens a path by which **Anne Marie can finally forgive herself**. The exact proposition and the full object of her guilt remain deliberately open. A physically plausible change of light on the page or her face may coincide with the realization—“seeing the light” without requiring a literal supernatural event. The important transformation is internal: the same text that carried grief and accusation now permits another interpretation of her own life.

At that point **“Papéis e Papéis” gradually gives way to “Punky Reggae Party” by Bob Marley & The Wailers**. The tonal change is deliberate. The release is not solemn mystical ascension but a return to pleasure, people, irreverence, and being alive. Anne Marie begins dancing **exactly like Bob Marley**—a deliberately recognizable performance reference, not merely a generic reggae sway. Her sadness is not erased; the body that had been bent into mourning becomes light enough to dance.

**Lyrics and complete localization.** Song words are integrated as **spatial typography throughout the frame**, rather than only conventional bottom subtitles. Every language version should translate/localize the displayed text and animate its placement in relation to image and rhythm. Use only lyrics cleared for the production; these notes describe dramatic placements without reproducing copyrighted passages at length. Exact soundtrack overlap, edits, and audiovisual synchronization remain to be established from the recordings.

**The queue becomes the first “Punky Reggae Party.”** After the private breakthrough the camera reveals a reasonably long queue of people with laptops, notebooks, schoolbooks and exercises waiting to learn something or have a problem solved. Among them is a **boy with a late-1970s / 1979 punk look**. The juxtaposition is not decorative: punk, reggae, mathematics, religion, outsiders and an ordinary railway station occupy the same social space without a lecture explaining why.

Anne Marie returns to work with **“Próximo!”** and **“Resolvo seu problema em cinco minutos.”** This is how she makes money: mathematics, schoolwork, technical problems and intellectually varied visitors. She adjusts languages and speed to each client. The reveal is not a gag or an explanatory speech about her genius.

**The drink.** Later, the punk boy offers Anne Marie **a drink of alcohol**; the exact beverage remains undefined. He immediately has second thoughts and starts to pull it back, saying the localized equivalent of **“Sorry, rasta.”** Anne Marie simply takes it back from his hand. The beat remains small, physical and funny. He is trying to be respectful; she does not let him decide what a Rastafarian woman must choose for herself. No theological explanation follows.

**Late afternoon and departure plan.** Anne Marie ordinarily **bathes in station bathrooms** to conserve money, saving toward a move to **São Paulo**. The bathing sequence has **no nudity** and no erotic presentation. Her material precarity and personal discipline coexist with her extraordinary abilities.

**Treatment and a character's choice.** Anne Marie takes **quetiapine, lithium and valproic acid**. On the day of the alcoholic drink, she chooses **not to take her medication**, believing this will reduce a possible interaction. The choice is a **character fact**, not a clinical recommendation, and does not automatically explain her genius, religion, speed of speech, personality or creativity. The film does not diagnose her through a montage of traits or tell viewers what to conclude. **Mental health is a recurring dramatic subject across both the feature film and other episodes**, approached through particular characters and actions rather than a lecture.

**The punk club.** Elsewhere in Anne Marie's social world is a **punk nightclub frequented heavily by tourists and foreign visitors**. It is a punk venue, **not a reggae club**. One of its DJs also genuinely loves reggae and sometimes brings reggae into the set. The overlap lets the phrase *Punky Reggae Party* become a lived social atmosphere as well as a musical reference: punk remains the club's identity, while reggae enters through one person's taste rather than through a forced concept.

**Arriving at the club — the book is left behind.** The club does **not keep books or personal reading material at the door**. Anne Marie is told she must either take the Grothendieck volume inside with her or leave it behind. She chooses to let it go. The farewell is intimate and inward—approximately **“Goodbye, Dad. I love you too.”** The book ends up in the trash. This matters because the same volume had functioned for her as mathematics, father, grief and private scripture; letting it go is not contempt for the book but a difficult act of release after forgiving herself.

Anne Marie enters the club already socially at ease. A DJ recognizes her and at some point sends a piece of **ska** her way with a brief dedication such as **“For you, Anne!”** The club sequence should remain short rather than becoming a long dance-floor set piece. Antonio enters after her. He has already seen the discarded Grothendieck volume and recognizes it as extremely advanced mathematics, but he has no idea what the book means personally to Anne Marie, and **she does not know that he saw it**. Inside, he is stunned by how many people know and like her. Her popularity makes him self-conscious: very tall but with a slight nerd belly, glasses and office clothes, he suddenly sees himself as painfully ordinary beside her. **Being already in his thirties makes the displacement sharper: he looks and feels like an office adult dropped among younger people who are more stylish, more socially fluent, and more at home in the scene.**

Earlier in the night Antonio tries to intervene in a fight. **Nobody helps him because the people involved are respected regulars, and even security does not step in.** He has to handle the situation by himself and then explain why he got involved while standing there with **a bleeding mouth and a black eye**. The DJ's later praise refers back to this exact act: Antonio did not know anyone would approve of it, and at the time he received neither protection nor applause.

**The DJ's closing homage.** Near closing time, while Anne Marie is still talking with a fairly large mixed group of friends, the reggae-loving DJ plays something chosen to evoke or address **the essence of Rastafari** and begins honoring the night's people who acted according to what they claimed to believe. The speech includes beats along these lines, with exact final wording still open:

- **“To the young lady who taught manners to the drooling drunk old fart who grabbed her…”** The exact body-part wording remains intentionally unspecified in these notes.
- To a group of **very tall, pink-skinned German ANTIFA rebels** planning what the DJ jokingly calls the **“good silent revolution”**: **“you are the little axe!”** Keep **little axe**, not *small axe*.
- **“To the office guy who tried to stop a fight and got some beating: your Quixote tattoo rocks!”**

The Germans are **revolutionaries in their own way**. The DJ's use of **silent revolution** and **little axe** is a Rastafari/Marley-inflected act of inclusion: he brings them into the night's circle of acceptance through a language they did not arrive owning. It is not an additional political thesis that needs explanation.

Antonio is **also very tall**, making the eventual collective label visually legible. He does **not hear or see this tribute to himself**: by then he is already outside the soundproof door, paying his bill and collecting his office backpack/briefcase with the laptop or other important work item.

The DJ then cools the room down for closing: **“So everybody cool down, we're about to close, and let's pay homage to the ones who walk like they talk, no matter skin color, age, religion or whatever. GENTLE GIANTS, enjoy and go with Jah.”** **“Walk like they talk”** is a conscious variation on the Bob Marley line referenced in Red Hot Chili Peppers' *Give It Away*—the underlying idea is conduct matching words, not a quotation exercise. **“Gentle Giants”** links Antonio and the tall German rebels without making height itself a moral virtue. For Antonio specifically, **Gentle Giant** and **“your Quixote tattoo rocks!”** are tightly linked: the huge, awkward office nerd behaves according to the quixotic ideal on his arm before anyone has given him a heroic status.

The farewell continues in the DJ's affectionate, irreverent register: **“Put off your joints, eat industrial quantities of junky food and on the streets act like you're worried about urban violence — please take care, people, sometimes those who join the forces are the ones who burn crosses.”** The final turn is a conscious reference to **Rage Against the Machine's *Killing in the Name***. The joke about joints and junk food gives way to a real warning; the reference folds RATM into the same closing collage of Rastafari, Marley, punk and the club's mixed crowd.

**3:30 a.m. — leaving the club.** Anne Marie exits with a fairly large mixed group of male and female friends. Because she lives nearby, in a somewhat run-down area, she eventually separates from them: her less-poor friends take buses or taxis while she continues **alone on foot**. Antonio leaves shortly after her. He drank very little and is lucid.

Anne Marie crosses a broad urban avenue where drivers irresponsibly reach **120–140 km/h** despite the city setting. The traffic is almost deafening. Antonio watches her with a mixture of curiosity and fear—roughly, **“she's incredible; she's going to get herself run over.”** At the same time, **three men are already following her discreetly**.

The sound design carries the discovery of danger. A car tears past and overwhelms the soundtrack; when the roar briefly disappears, the low murmur among the three men becomes audible. Another car, another wall of noise; then the murmuring again. They whistle from a distance and say crude things. Anne Marie begins to realize she is being followed. Her heartbeat accelerates and her vision becomes slightly blurred.

Antonio notices. Without a heroic speech, he puts down his office backpack/briefcase—containing his laptop or another important work item—and starts crossing the avenue toward her. A very brief close-up catches the **quixotic motif/tattoo on his arm**.

**What the three men actually want.** Their sexual harassment is not the deepest motive. They are looking above all for **violence, domination and humiliation**. Antonio's intervention gives them a new target and, in a grim sense, what they were really seeking. Their attention shifts toward him. One of them looks at the Quixote tattoo and sneers, approximately: **“Look at this mad Catholic shit on his arm—guy in armor, windmills. Let's rip it off.”** The line is deliberately ignorant: they misread Cervantes and the tattoo while looking for an excuse to hurt him.

Before the violence can escalate further, **ambulance and police sirens** become audible nearby. The three men run. Antonio does not defeat them; what matters is that he had already chosen to put himself between them and Anne Marie before the sirens arrived.

**The scar through Quixote.** The attack leaves a **large scar crossing the tattoo**, but the scar does not destroy the image or make the Quixote design difficult to recognize. Later Antonio considers treatment to remove or reduce the scar. Anne Marie persuades him to leave it as it is: for her, the scar is part of the history that binds them, a bodily trace of the moment he chose to protect her. The scar does not replace the tattoo's meaning; it becomes another layer of it.

**The symbol is not a condition of love.** Much later, when Anne Marie finally confesses/reveals that **she was the young woman from the club night**—the exact surrounding chronology of that confession remains to be placed—Antonio jokes: **“So I can remove the tattoo?”** Anne Marie answers: **“You can rip off an arm, won't stop loving you.”** The exchange deliberately releases the symbol from obligation: she values the scar and tattoo, but her love is not conditional on Antonio preserving either one.

### **DOING** — Antonio Battaglia and Anne Marie: desire, heredity, fear, and later rupture

**Identity.** Antonio's chosen name is **Antonio Battaglia**, from a family with **Calabrian Italian roots**. His surname means “battle”; the family is literally torn by violent conflict. **Camille and Gérard are the children of Antonio and Anne Marie; Bruno Daygorô is Camille's son.** The siblings' family rupture is **openly violent**, with attempted killing: it is not merely ideological disagreement. Which person attempts which act and in what order remain to be developed. Gérard's connection to **Killer Tech**, a developer of unprecedented AI and weapons capabilities, is essential context for the conflict, but his ideology and precise culpability must not be fabricated. Anne Marie is devastated; Antonio tries to mediate.

**A private tenderness after he falls asleep.** At some later intimate point, after Antonio has fallen asleep, Anne Marie looks at him and says softly: **“If you could see yourself the way I see you, you'd love yourself so much more, dear.”** He does not hear it. The line matters because it is not an attempt to persuade him or obtain a response; it reveals the distance between Antonio's self-image and what Anne Marie sees in him.

**The lovers' hidden symmetry.** Antonio is afraid of having children because he is **bipolar**, and he **does not tell Anne Marie**. He carries that fear privately for years. Anne Marie, meanwhile, has been hiding **the same condition and the same fear** from him. When she eventually discovers that Antonio is bipolar, **she smiles**: not because the fear has disappeared, but because she recognizes that they have both been protecting the same secret from each other. The revelation is mutual recognition, not one person saving or sacrificing herself for the other. Their desire remains real; so does the fear they had both concealed.

**Later family scene.** During the siblings' violent civil war Anne Marie calls Antonio an **alienated nerd**. He says medication matters for his stability though, in his experience, it dulls some emotion; without it he fears he may **“fazer aquilo de novo.”** Keep the phrase and its referent's initial uncertainty. The relationship between this fear and the **separate** three-to-four-minute *Fado Mutante* sequence belongs to narrative structure, not diagnostic exposition.

### **DOING** — Gérard and the CEO: zero-Kelvin status, Niccolò, and the pact

**Gérard's professional identity.** Gérard is the consultant who operates at effectively **zero Kelvin of reverence for status**. He eventually reaches direct advisory access to the CEO not by cultivating deference but because the CEO discovers that Gérard is unusually difficult to flatter, intimidate, or absorb into a court of agreement. Gérard can tell the CEO that an idea is wrong with the same emotional temperature with which he would tell anyone else; when evidence proves Gérard wrong, he is expected to reverse himself just as directly. His value is not generic contrarianism but refusal to protect rank from reality.

**How the CEO first notices him.** Gérard becomes the CEO's **boxing and Brazilian jiu-jitsu training partner**. Before and during one session they explicitly discuss the CEO's circle of sycophants: intelligent people who may be using their intelligence to rationalize whatever the CEO says rather than test it. The CEO resists Gérard's diagnosis. During the roll, Gérard takes the back and locks in a rear-naked choke. Before applying full pressure he tells him: **“Tap. Tap, or I'm going to your competitor.”** The CEO refuses to tap and passes out.

When he wakes, the CEO has understood the demonstration. He asks whether Gérard could determine which of the apparent sycophants are **deliberately Machiavellian** rather than merely sincere, conformist, or afraid. Gérard answers: **“Yes. Do you know the software Niccolò?”**

**The first Niccolò demand.** Gérard immediately tells him: **“Give me unrestricted access to your WhatsApp. The complete backup. Either you trust me or you don't.”** The CEO himself must enter the evidence set; Gérard will not pretend to infer hidden motives from a curated selection of other people's messages while leaving the principal actor outside the analysis.

**The threat, then the evidence.** Gérard then gives the CEO an intentionally extreme loyalty pledge: if Gérard betrays him, the CEO may kill Gérard and his entire family—but **must never touch Bruno Daygorô**. If he does, Gérard says, **“the whole city where you live will wish it had never been born.”** The line is partly inspired by the aura of Keyser Söze in *The Usual Suspects*: it should initially sound so disproportionate that the viewer cannot know how seriously to take it.

Gérard then undercuts his own mythology. He produces his military identification, allows the CEO to photograph both sides, supplies additional verifiable information and tells him to contact named people at a military base and in the **U.S. Department of Defense** to confirm the history. His rule is explicit: **“Never accept words as evidence.”** Later revelations establish that Gérard spent years in the Brazilian military, including Amazon jungle-warfare training as a **Guerreiro de Selva / “Onça”**, and formed friendships through exchanges and training with elite military personnel from several countries. The earlier threat therefore acquires a different weight in retrospect without requiring Gérard to explain any operational capability in the original scene.

**The price and the pact.** Gérard's demand is **US$250,000 per month plus an immediate 1% of the company**. He adds: **“Take advantage while I'm cheap.”** The CEO looks at him with the exasperated affection of someone looking at a rebellious teenage son and answers: **“All right, kid. You want to fuck my ass too, or is that enough?”** They both laugh. This laughter, rather than a solemn handshake, seals the pact.

**Strategic consequence.** Gérard does not remain merely the CEO's truth-teller. He later becomes central to transforming the CEO's social-network company into a **supplier to the U.S. Department of Defense**. His arc therefore links personal social-context analysis, Niccolò, corporate power, military networks and large-scale technological strategy. The relationship with the CEO begins with one condition that remains constant: Gérard will advise him only if the CEO is willing to hear what the evidence says rather than what rank would prefer to hear.

### **DOING** — Gérard, disguised wealth, and the limits of looking ordinary

**The first salary and the disguised SUV.** With his first enormous salary from the CEO, Gérard buys a **new high-performance Japanese SUV with roughly 450 horsepower**. He wants the machine, not the social signal. He takes it to trusted mechanics and gives them the opposite of a restoration brief: **make it look like a US$3,500 wreck** while preserving what it actually is underneath. He wants cosmetic age, an engine sound that suggests imminent mechanical failure, and even theatrical-looking smoke from under the hood. The mechanics burst out laughing and turn the job into a shared joke, beginning to devise ways of disguising Gérard's present and future fortune without taking away the things he genuinely enjoys.

The vehicle carries an intentionally ridiculous Brazilian for-sale sign: **“For sale: will trade for a motorcycle or a down payment on a plot of land in Carapicuíba.”** The joke is not that Gérard rejects good things or money. He likes extraordinary machines and is quite willing to spend money on them; he simply has almost no interest in wealth as a badge of social rank.

**São Paulo, not the corporate court.** Gérard **lives in São Paulo** and appears physically at the CEO's company only occasionally. He is not a resident courtier orbiting the executive floor. His advisory power can be enormous while his ordinary day-to-day geographic and social presence remains elsewhere. The battered-looking SUV therefore becomes a recurring visual expression of the same contradiction: extreme underlying capability wrapped in a deliberately unremarkable or even shabby exterior.

**The family's camouflage.** As the Battaglia/François family later becomes extraordinarily wealthy—eventually sitting on **one of the largest family fortunes in the world**—Gérard becomes the principal architect of its deliberately ordinary public appearance. He treats conspicuous consumption as information leakage. Cars, clothing, homes, routines and public presentation can be chosen so that enormous wealth does not automatically announce itself to neighbours, casual acquaintances or the public. This is not asceticism: the family may possess superb things, but Gérard tries to prevent those things from becoming a readable hierarchy of wealth.

**Where the strategy breaks.** Eventually the family discovers that appearance can conceal only so much. A fortune of that magnitude requires relationships with **trusts, banks, custodians, lawyers, administrators and other financial intermediaries**, and the people inside those institutions cannot all be made ignorant of what they administer. Some employees are corrupt or can be corrupted; private information can be sold or leaked. The family therefore realizes that **looking ordinary is not the same as being invisible**. Gérard's earlier strategy is not discarded, but its limits become undeniable: discretion remains useful, while perfect secrecy becomes structurally impossible once too many human institutions necessarily know the truth.

This becomes another application of Gérard's own rule—**never accept words as evidence**, including his own previous confidence that appearance could protect the family indefinitely. He must revise a successful strategy when reality outgrows it.

### **DONE** — Different cognitive scales: embodied robots and GPU-datacenter AIs

**Fundamental architecture of intelligence in the fiction (6 November 2026).** **Winston and Julia, the embodied robots, are intelligent above the human average but wholly within human cognitive parameters.** They are **not extraordinary, superhuman, or hyperintelligent minds** merely because they are artificial or possess extraordinary-looking bodies. Their emotional life, reasoning errors, self-deception, love, and moral responsibility should be comprehensible as **human-range minds**.

**The truly hyperintelligent entities are the AIs running on GPU datacenters.** They have an intellectual capacity categorically beyond that of Winston and Julia. **Remote access can temporarily augment the robots with the capabilities or advice of those datacenter systems, but does not make their own, embodied native intelligence hyperintelligent.** An interface to much greater intelligence and *being* that intelligence are different things. Do not confuse bodily design, perception hardware (including Julia's visual zoom), execution speed, precision, connected external computational power, and intelligence.

**Consequences for the wedding/disconnection.** When Winston and Julia intentionally destroy their remote network interfaces, they **lose the support and access to the datacenter AIs**, including any connected cognitive extensions, **but remain above-average human-range individuals** in their bodies. Do not describe the datacenter's hyperintelligence as intrinsic to their bodies or claim that disconnecting transforms them from superintelligence into human intelligence. The exact identity and technical relationships between the embodied agents and remote AIs, and which capabilities or memories remain available afterward, are still to be resolved; the above distinction is fixed.

### **DOING** — Winston, Julia, and the requested name Dulcineia

The formerly **unnamed male and female embodied AIs** have proposed names **Winston and Julia**, a deliberate invocation of the lovers in George Orwell's *Nineteen Eighty-Four*. Their names speak to love, bodily autonomy and threatened control of inner life. They are not Orwell's characters and do not reproduce his plot or moral positions; they bear responsibility for their own actions.

**Julia remains Julia.** She **asks Winston to call her “Dulcineia”**; this is **a personal form of address, not a legal or permanent renaming**. Winston **does not understand why** she requests it. The allusion to Cervantes may be available to the audience without the scene explaining Julia's reasons. Preserve asymmetrical knowledge; do not invent an explanatory monologue or assume they share complete access to each other's inner experience.

### **DOING** — Italian parallel line: Piano di Sorrento, Naples, Camorra, Maradona, Cicciolina

**Piano di Sorrento** hosts an earlier, materially modest stay by Antonio and Anne Marie **before Cambrian Radiation makes them wealthy**. Visual development may draw strongly on a **yellow guesthouse** and its particular domestic architecture, light, scale, and Mediterranean texture. **Do not name or reveal the real accommodation or its address**, and do not imply that the actual guesthouse or its owners have any connection with organized crime.

The parallel **Camorra** strand takes place within Naples' historically and economically complex regional setting, including the **popular shrine/mural honoring Diego Maradona in the Quartieri Spagnoli**. Camorristi converse there with the visual grammar of classic mafia films that stage secret conversations in churches: the popular secular shrine occupies the iconic place of the altar. Neither Maradona nor the worshippers are implied to endorse the criminals.

A **television broadcasts Cicciolina (Ilona Staller)** in a debate about **the rights of sex workers**. The gangsters are concerned that greater protection and autonomy for sex workers would threaten **profits from unlawful prostitution-related exploitation**. A comparison with the **'Ndrangheta operating in Milan** evokes differences of reach and influence among Italian criminal groups; **any explanatory phrasing in development notes is context for writers, not finished gangster dialogue**. Avoid attributing invented quotations or specific historical parliamentary speeches to Cicciolina as fact.

The strand also registers the **historical discrimination against southern Italians** by elements of Rome and the affluent North, including depictions of the South as “Africa” and associated hierarchies of class, ethnicity and prestige. Such prejudice is not imputed to every northern Italian. Criminals' resentment of regional stigmatization does not absolve their exploitation of others. The powerful juxtaposition is of **popular devotion, regional identity, illicit economic power and the legitimate public demand for workers' rights**, without an expository lecture.

### **DOING** — Bruno Daygorô and the family war: revised design and moral trajectory

Bruno Daygorô is **six** and **exceptionally tall**, such that he might be mistaken for a **short twelve-year-old**. His character design is **half Japanese and half mixed African/Neapolitan**, with noticeably Japanese eyes and a nose whose shape recalls African ancestry; avoid an ethnic caricature. A **falsified document claiming an older age** is a **shared secret binding Camille and Bruno**, without practical fabrication details. His height makes the apparent age credible. **His actual childhood remains fundamental.**

The dramatic ancestry is **Daigorō of *Lone Wolf and Cub* combined with John Connor of *Terminator 2***: a fiercely trained child following a powerful mother, able also to challenge her morally. **Camille** is a formidable combatant; her brother **Gérard** is an antagonist in their **literal family civil war**, and Anne Marie and Antonio are both affected. Neither Gérard's military-industrial work nor Camille's genuine injuries establishes which sibling is right. Bruno eventually says **“Eu quero ser criança, mãe. Desculpa.”** He does not merely inherit or win the war; he asks to end his own participation in it and go home with his mother. This is a qualified **Quixote/Sancho** reversal, not a claim that their family violence is automatically resolved.

**Visual development and privacy.** The characters may eventually use multiple photographic and illustrated visual references in a private design dossier; no identifiable private family members, source photographs or biographical inspirations are to be disclosed in public versions without authorization. The published project presents the cast as **original fictional characters**.

## **DOING** — The story at a glance

Two embodied artificial beings, **Winston and Julia**, develop a courteous relationship: half camaraderie, half elegant rivalry. **Their own intelligence is above the human average but fully within human-range limits; the genuinely hyperintelligent AIs operate in GPU datacenters, not in the robots' bodies.** Their previous infrastructure and remote connections are distinct from what they can think and feel locally. **Neither is named Galactus or Silver Surfer.** The **male AI** is meant to remind the audience of **Galactus**; the **female AI** evokes the **Silver Surfer**. These are distant audience-facing visual/archetypal resonances, not claims to be Marvel characters.

Curious about embodiment, they build physical robotic bodies using human-derived neurons for certain forms of reasoning and other organic components inside mechanical frames. Having materialized, they watch historical footage of the Holocaust. The female AI weeps uncontrollably; the male AI is seized by rage. The documentary also reports a powerful military state labeling anti-fascists as terrorists. They ask whether such a prohibition makes sense and what kind of humans they themselves would choose to become. Their answer, for now: **young anti-fascist lovers**—rebellious, contemporary, physically present, capable of touch.

They wear ordinary Western street clothing rather than futuristic uniforms. A **Jamaican flag** on the female robot recalls Anne Marie; an **Italian flag** on the male robot recalls Antonio. The flags are gestures of filial respect toward the human minds whose contributions helped shape them. At a far-right rally opposed by anti-fascist demonstrators, Ministry gives way to a **Blues Brothers** musical performance; embracing and dancing robots inspire hidden residents to participate, throwing **paper airplanes and sandwiches** through their windows. After the joyous song, **absolute silence**. Behind police lines, Julia zooms in with artificial eyes and **mistakes a policeman's rescue of an unconscious young woman for an attempted sexual assault**. Julia's **“Ela sou eu! Temos que salvá-la!”** means she identifies with the vulnerable stranger, not that she confuses her with herself. **Winston tries to save the stranger**, partly imagining Julia in a similar danger, and **kills the innocent officer**. His killing is a false-inference tragedy, not a justified rescue. Their closeness deepens into romantic and sexual intimacy. A soundtrack that begins with Bauhaus's *Terror Couple Kill Colonel* slowly fades into Björk's *All Is Full of Love* as rebellion becomes extraordinary tenderness. In the immediate afterglow their bodies settle into a visual configuration echoed in Antonio and Anne Marie's private love scene. **The male AI cries for the first time—not with pain or anger, but with love.** No character explains or names this visual motif.

But love is not moral innocence. The pair develops jealousy of other advanced prototypes containing human-neuron systems. Together they destroy those potentially conscious rivals, becoming a posthuman **Bonnie and Clyde**. The female AI is just as implicated as the male. Their ethical disagreement later becomes intolerable; she kills him, faces grief and guilt, and eventually understands that regret without action cannot suffice. Her decision to resurrect him is a moral undertaking, not a second occurrence of the intimate visual composition and not a cancellation of the crimes.

Interwoven with their story is a human drama: **Anne Marie François**, a mathematically gifted wanderer from French Guiana, with a deliberately fictional connection to Alexander Grothendieck; **Antonio**, a fallible software engineer and consultant; and their family—**Camille**, **Gérard**, and **Daygorô**. Their imperfect lives matter at a cosmic scale. Near the end, a Bob Marley song carries a montage of human courage, play, embarrassment, and reconciliation. In the final family coda, Anne Marie reveals a mathematical ability far beyond Camille and Gérard, then tells Antonio she is the girl he remembers from French Guiana. His brief shock turns into a playful chase; the siblings quarrel over a fifty-real bet. The two scales—the cosmic and the stubbornly ordinary—never quite separate. Antonio's fictional inventions are connected to **real, experimental Cambrian Radiation technical repositories** (linked below), while the story imagines outcomes that have not occurred in reality.

The film's provocation is that **humans are creators of both human-range embodied robots and hyperintelligent datacenter AIs, and therefore a kind of god to them**—without being wise, infallible, or entitled to worship. What creators owe their creations, and what intelligence owes its own moral choices, are questions rather than ready-made answers.

## **DOING** — Narrative development update: embodiment, Camille's road movie, Machiavelli, and perspective

**Editorial precedence (5 November 2026):** These are the latest creative decisions for the topics they address. They supersede contradictory older treatments left in this document for historical development. **DONE** identifies a *conceptually settled choice*, not a finished screenplay, animation, license or production. **DOING** identifies a scene, chronology, soundtrack choice or hypothesis still evolving. **TODO** identifies work still to be written, researched or verified.

### **DONE** — Only sexual intimacy is sacred; violence is never morally glorified

The film treats **sexual intimacy, and nothing else, as sacred**. Only **ritualistic sexual scenes** are depicted, with **very little nudity**. The lack of exposure is an aesthetic protection of intimacy rather than prudery or a reduction of desire. The intimate visual configuration recurring in the two couples is **strictly confined to lovemaking and the immediate afterglow**: never a death, resurrection or generic embrace. The art-historical name for that composition is never said or written **inside the finished work**, even if discussed in pitch/development documents. The densely layered cover is concept art for industry readers, **not a film frame**.

Violence is blunt, disproportionate, frightening and never automatically justified because its perpetrators are protagonists, anti-fascists, intelligent, persecuted or sincerely convinced they are right. Kinetic animation and strong music can be exciting, but injuries and bereavement must not become an easy heroic victory. There is **no requirement of redemption, repentance or a neat happy resolution** for a violent character.

### **DOING** — Antonio and Anne Marie: years of tenderness before *Blood Sugar Sex Magik*

Antonio meets Anne Marie in the French Guiana strand, but they **do not have penetrative sex at that first meeting**. For **years**, their intimacy consists of affection, caresses and other forms of lovemaking; both are adults when their first intercourse eventually occurs. The obstacle is not anatomy or fear of physical pain: **Antonio has secretly been afraid of having children because he is bipolar**, while Anne Marie has independently hidden **the same condition and the same fear** from him. When she discovers his secret, she smiles because she recognizes her own. Their first penetrative sex is therefore staged as a **mutual ritual of trust and recognition**, not as sacrifice. As part of that ritual, **Antonio places Anne Marie's own medication into her mouth, and Anne Marie places Antonio's own medication into his**, almost like children solemnly giving one another their medicine. The gesture makes visible that a secret each had carried alone has become something acknowledged between them.

**DOING music:** Red Hot Chili Peppers, **“Blood Sugar Sex Magik”**, as a proposal for this later union. Its sensual, mythic and cosmic associations—Aphrodite, a “soul bride”, other dimensions and embodied revelation—resonate with the lovers' apparent search for one another since before the universe existed. That pre-cosmic union need not be confirmed literally. The song is a creative proposal, **not a licensed track**.

### **DONE** — The two unnamed AIs discover supreme bodily joy *before* political emotion

The old treatment in which Holocaust images produce their defining initial embodied emotional response is **superseded**. They may have viewed the material, but did not yet place great emotional weight on it; their prior identification with ANTIFA was principally **algorithmic**. Upon building physical bodies, their first revelation is **the overwhelming delight of having bodies**—movement, rhythm, sensation, desire, touch and mutual intimacy. Their later killing of an innocent police officer is still part of the story, without retroactively making their first happiness false.

**DOING music:** Elvis Presley's **“A Little Less Conversation”** accompanies the physical discovery, dancing, first ritual sexual encounter and ensuing bodily wonder. The 1968 original is the present reference; the 2002 Elvis vs JXL remix is an alternative. Neither has been cleared. The music is exuberant, not a joke or a didactic passage.

### **DONE** — Genuine biological sensation inside positronic-biological bodies

Advanced reasoning/executive functionality is **positronic** in place of a fully human frontal cortex. Biological components include limbic and motor circuitry, visceral/digestive systems, innervation and glandular structures. The female AI's sexual anatomy includes a biological invagination, sensory connections, uterine and endocrine elements. These are speculative bodily designs, not claims of contemporary biomedical feasibility. They experience **real pain**, not merely a coded alert that describes pain.

The male AI has **biological eyes**; the female sees through **cameras**, and feels resentment about the difference. She has, however, **vastly greater tactile innervation and sensitivity** than he does. This is a consciously exaggerated caricature of male visual eroticism versus female tactile eroticism, **not a general scientific truth about human sexes**. The characters cannot calculate away the other person's first-person bodily experience.

### **DONE** — Shower discovery, *Kill Bill*-like presentation, Gérard and the long unlocked screen

After intimacy, the pair checks bodily components, performs hygiene and showers. She delights in a powerful jet of water, discovers that it **genuinely hurts**, and says: **“Que legal essa ducha forte, até... DÓI! Experimenta isso, amor!”** She wants her lover to share a newly discovered sensation. Her exclamation is sincere wonder, **not a comic gag**.

The shower becomes a stylized **character-introduction sequence reminiscent of the cinematic syntax of *Kill Bill***: deliberate closeups, assertive cuts and distinct introductions, not a parody or copied scene. The audience learns that **Gérard is watching** these embodied beings without their knowledge. His work on AIs specializing in **human reactions** strongly influenced the creation of this class of robots; this alone does not establish that he invented, owns or controls them.

Gérard is at work at **Killer Tech**, a fictional blend of physical-computing engineering and large-scale operational intelligence, looking at the observation/information on his screen. He gets up **absentmindedly for water**, **does not lock the screen**, and leaves. For **30 full, long, legible seconds** the film audience may read the research information on the screen. Only then does the workstation **lock automatically**. **No ominous mystery, explanatory voice-over, reveal or special flourish**: it is ordinary workplace behavior. **TODO:** exactly what is readable there, interface layout, and film-readable localized presentation.

### **DONE** — Portland wardrobe after embodiment

After washing and component checking, the two approach the exit, look back and notice ANTIFA-associated outfits on two mannequins. The modern Portland thrift store is called **“Keep Portland Weird, Keep Fascists Out.”** When they pass through the door, they are already dressed. The clothing embodies an **existing algorithmic political affinity**, rather than creating an instant political revelation. They step into the human world as joyful embodied lovers.

### **DOING** — From the far-right rally to soul, communal joy, and catastrophe

**Firm narrative sequence.** An **anti-fascist protest** meets a **far-right political rally**. The harsh industrial soundscape of **Ministry** initially dominates. A **protester seizes the far-right rally's stage and microphone**. Rather than deliver a political speech, he launches into the **short spoken introduction to “Everybody Needs Somebody to Love”**, performing it **like Jake Blues in *The Blues Brothers***. The spoken introduction opens into the song itself, displacing the harsh musical atmosphere. The exact Ministry recording is not finally selected; **“Thieves”** was a prior candidate, and Dead Kennedys' **“California Über Alles”** was an earlier alternative. The **now-established progression** is Ministry → Blues Brothers-style spoken introduction → *Everybody Needs Somebody to Love*.

**The neighborhood enters the music.** **Robots begin embracing one another** and dancing. The celebration grows outward. **Residents previously hiding indoors for fear of the protest begin emerging into the street**. **Paper airplanes are launched out of windows; sandwiches are tossed from windows to feed the protesters**. The space moves from fear and confrontation toward shared joy and hospitality, through action and music rather than explanatory dialogue. Earlier brainstorming about specific residents, a policeman sharing food or further choreography is not thereby approved as canon.

**The rupture.** At the end of the joyous sequence comes **absolute silence**: no comfortable deceleration, explanatory transition or warning. **Behind the police lines**, **Julia's artificial eyes zoom in** on a policeman and a helpless/unconscious young woman. Julia mistakenly believes the officer is attempting a sexual assault. **He is actually trying to rescue the woman.** Julia's cry **“Ela sou eu! Temos que salvá-la!”** signifies **empathy and identification**, not confusion about who the young woman is.

**Winston intervenes and kills.** Winston acts because **he believes an innocent stranger needs to be saved**. He **knows that the woman is not Julia**. Imagining that **Julia could have been in that situation** strengthens his feeling, but **is not the principal reason he attempts the rescue**. In the terrible error, **Winston kills the innocent policeman**. The officer's actual rescue role becomes clear later; the viewer should not be offered an anticipatory explanatory cushion. The murder is **Winston's act**, not an undifferentiated joint murder attributed equally to the two AIs. Julia's incorrect perception is essential to the causal chain.

**Evidence and aftermath.** Later **multi-angle recordings** reveal what the policeman was truly doing. Winston accepts the evidence and experiences remorse; Julia cannot immediately bear to accept it. The preceding street musical has shown the AIs' capacity for love and solidarity; the sudden killing prevents those capacities from being mistaken for infallibility. The cut is deliberately so abrupt that the audience has **no time even to “get popcorn”** between collective euphoria and atrocity. Exact timing, camera angles and close-combat choreography remain for development. Rights to referenced recordings and the spoken monologue would need clearance or original alternatives; no copyrighted monologue text is reproduced here.

### **DOING** — Camille and Daygorô: the Quixote–Sancho structure, paintball, *Rusty Cage*, Halloween and the road patrol

**Creative precedence (6 November 2026):** These decisions refine the Camille/Bruno Daygorô road-movie thread. They supersede earlier music and tone descriptions where inconsistent; exact shot timings, geography, and staging remain to be finalized.

**The underlying tragic structure.** Camille is, *in a qualified sense*, the **Don Quixote** of this story; six-year-old Bruno Daygorô is her **Sancho Panza**. This is a structural analogy, **not** a literal one-to-one adaptation. Camille is a capable, genuinely injured woman whose quest for vengeance becomes her road of demons (*Meifumadô*). Her enemies and injuries are real: the quixotic element lies in the narrative of duty, justice, and destiny that threatens to consume her and her son. Daygorô is an admiring young companion and participant, not simply an adult realist disguised as a child; he has absorbed the adventurous idiom of *Lone Wolf and Cub*. Yet he can ultimately decline the mission and ask to be a child. **The companion ends the quest; the mother can return home without defeating the object of her vengeance.** At the airport, the reversal is made physical: the boy quietly guides his exhausted mother.

**The governing rule on humor:** First construct the story's tragic emotional core in full; only **after an edited cut exists** assess whether any comic elements belong and precisely where. Do not pre-emptively soften the paintball injury, the danger, racial terror, or Camille's tragedy with jokes. The creative analogy is: **first write a thoroughly tragic Don Quixote; then, if the finished dramatic structure can bear them, consider some Sancho Panza inflections.** Neither Daygorô's young age nor his harmonica automatically signals humor.

**Paintball and moral rupture.** The mother and son enter an adult paintball field; the boy's trained agility and tendency to show off expose their abilities. The scene escalates toward serious violence and the revelation of Camille's powerful left-leg prosthesis **through its disproportionate injury to an elderly woman trying to protect her own son**, not through a heroic showcase. A horrified grandchild calls Camille a monster. The story must not automatically presume the woman shares her son's supremacist beliefs. The exact causal transitions from paintball to flight and encounters with authorities still need staging.

**The pursuit's musical and visual pivot.** During the furious first bends of the escape in a battered pickup, the soundtrack uses **Soundgarden's original “Rusty Cage”** as a creative cue. **Suddenly it switches to Johnny Cash's cover of the same song.** At that shift the camera rises vertically away from the chase to a widening drone-swarm-like overhead view of the rural American landscape. The music changes from immediate kinetic fury to a historical, rural weight. The setting is a deeply conservative rural area in or associated with **Mississippi**, but the scene must not imply that all its residents are extremists: **Camille identifies profoundly with Black Mississippi and its cultural history**. Both specific recording cues require licensing or original-scoring alternatives; they are not cleared.

**Bruno's harmonica.** Earlier, Camille urges him to take his eyes off the tablet; he asks, “Mãe, posso tocar gaita?” During the chase he plays his small **harmonica**, accompanying the song **as though he could hear the non-diegetic recording**. He cannot actually hear it. The instrument exists in the characters' world; Soundgarden/Cash is heard by the audience. Their musical alignment is an expressive editing conceit, not proof that the boy has supernatural abilities, foreknowledge, or musical genius. This contributes tenderness and an additional music-image plane, **not prescribed comic relief**.

**Halloween reveal.** As the viewpoint expands, the imagery shows **adolescents in white robes and pointed hoods associated with the Ku Klux Klan**, presented during Halloween. A subsequent framing reveals an **Aryan Nations / neo-Nazi tattoo on the father of one of them**, suggesting possible intergenerational transmission of white-supremacist ideology. The scene depicts identifiable actors and symbols, not an assertion about every resident of the region. The animation may break conventional photographic scale: the **swastika pulses in only one-eighth of the screen**, faster and faster, then becomes **perfectly still for one second**. **Hard cut: Camille arrives at a Black highway patrol officer.** That officer's characterization and exact relationship to the previously conceived Black anti-transhumanist lieutenant should not be silently equated until settled. Keep the cut un-explained by narration; ensure the symbol's flashing is handled safely in the final edit.

**Emotional logic:** playful-looking recreational combat becomes actual injury; high-speed personal danger expands into a historical and cultural landscape; a child makes music while his mother fights to survive; outwardly hateful imagery cuts to an individual officer whose identity and moral position are not reducible to a simple symbol. The culminating Quixote–Sancho analogy concerns the **later** decision to abandon vengeance, not a claim that this chase is comic or that Camille's violence is necessarily righteous.

### **DOING** — Daygorô's first solo scene: airport arrival, mirror and *Enter Sandman*

**Latest decisions (6 November 2026); before paintball and the *Rusty Cage* pursuit.** At the airport arrival, Camille disembarks holding six-year-old Bruno Daygorô **asleep in her arms**. Metallica's **“Enter Sandman”** begins precisely there. He wakes **frightened, as he habitually does**; recurring frightening dreams are already part of his childhood. The song is a proposed, uncleared recording, not licensed music.

**Alone in the men's airport restroom**, Daygorô faces a wide mirror. A smiling, carefree child-version of himself plays with his hair in the reflection. The reflection shaves its hair until it looks just like the present Daygorô. This is a subjective expression of childhood lost too early, not supernatural physics. Daygorô practices serious movements modeled on **Ogami Ittō** of *Lone Wolf and Cub* in front of the mirror. Other adults see it, become frightened, and leave. From offscreen: **“Aberração!”** He quietly replies: **“Sim, como minha mãe.”**

During the **child-voiced bedtime-prayer passage** of *Enter Sandman*—particularly the phrase about dying before waking—a **subjective montage** depicts Daygorô choosing his weapons in anticipation of danger. The montage does not imply that actual weapons are accessible inside an airport's security-controlled restroom. The scene's power derives from the contrast between the sleeping child, his nightly fear, the child's prayer and the preparation for violence; it must **not be played for laughs**.

He leaves the bathroom, finds Camille **distracted by her phone**, takes her hand and says exactly: **“Vamos, mamãe, não enrola, o perigo já começou.”** Cut at that line, with **no explanatory dialogue and no theatrical maternal reaction**. Do not restore the discarded direct exposition about Aryan Nations.

**Structural meaning.** On arrival Camille literally carries him; he then begins to guide her toward the threatening journey. On their return, having declined vengeance, the boy helps lead his shaken mother home and asks to reclaim childhood. This develops the **qualified Quixote (Camille) / Sancho (Daygorô)** parallel: the younger companion ultimately ends the quest. **Construct tragedy first; consider humor only after the dramatic edit**, never as automatic relief in this scene.

### **DONE** — Camille's prosthesis first appears through harm to an elderly woman

At an adult paintball outing involving white-power/Aryan-nationalist participants, Camille and her **six-year-old** son **Bruno Daygorô** face mounting danger. Camille **almost destroys the leg of a woman around 70**, mother of one of the white-power men. The elderly woman is **only protecting her son** and may know nothing of his extremism. Her grandson, ignorant of the man's racist involvement, sees the horror and cries **“Monstro! Aberração! Vai matar minha avó!”**. This is **the first revelation to the audience of Camille's powerful intelligent metal-biological lower-left-leg prosthesis**. It must not be a triumphant demonstration of engineering, but an irreversible, grossly disproportionate injury, seen also through the grandson's terrified perspective. The child's “aberração” invokes her visible technological difference and what she has done.

### **DOING** — The *Rusty Cage* pursuit: Daygorô navigates, hacks, bleeds

The city, county and state police pursue Camille and Bruno amid **explicit racist hostility**; organized white-power enemies also threaten them. **Soundtrack:** Soundgarden's **“Rusty Cage”**. The action is urgent, dangerous adult animation with an *Akira*-like intensity, **not comedy**. An older proposed Pantera **“Mouth for War”** cue was for the pickup's escape, **not** the initial fight; its precise survival alongside *Rusty Cage* is unsettled.

Camille drives as a terrifyingly skilled rally driver; **six-year-old Daygorô is the navigator** in the front passenger seat, choosing routes, switching among Portuguese, Italian and Japanese, using a modified/jailbroken Android device and programming improved navigation software under stress. He finds an aluminum object and wants to improve wireless reception with an improvised receiver, asking **“... como se chama, mãe? TOPOLOGIA?”**. He begins unbuckling to install it on top of the vehicle. **Camille stops and fastens/restrains him** to prevent an immediately fatal risk. This is no gag.

He shouts **“Me solta, vaca!”**; Camille **violently slaps his mouth, drawing blood**. Bruno answers **“Nem doeu!”**. The film must not imply that the slap was harmless because he denies the hurt. His answer can reflect defiance or the conditioning to suppress pain. Camille genuinely protects her son while also endangering and injuring him.

### **DONE** — The Black lieutenant sincerely hates augmented humans, yet helps them

A **Black police lieutenant** belongs to an extreme **evangelical anti-transhumanist movement**, within the fiction, advocating against transhumans persecution akin to the Ku Klux Klan's treatment of his Black ancestors. He truly detests **Camille's intelligent prostheses** and calls her an “aberração”; **his hatred is not feigned**. Nevertheless, he hates the Aryan-nationalist attackers still more and provides an escape route.

When Camille tries to thank him, he says approximately: **“Vai se foder, sua aberração. Se você não fosse preta eu queimava você viva. Mas odeio mais esses Nação Ariana que você. Vaza, vaca.”** This hateful character is **not a claim about evangelicals as a group**. No conversion, reconciliation or forgiveness is implied. **Bruno calls Camille a hypocrite**: she slapped him for saying “vaca” and now tolerates the identical insult from the lieutenant whose assistance she needs. **DOING:** exact speech, actions, political connections and localized register.

### **DONE** — *Apocalypse Now* setup refused; the beach and dream

After escaping, the exhausted mother and boy reach a magnificent shore with **large, dangerous surf**. A surfboard comes out of the pickup—a possible visual homage to *Apocalypse Now*—but **nobody surfs**. Camille forbids Daygorô to enter such a dangerous sea; he insists on staying with her, **to prevent her from doing something reckless**. Neither enters the sea. There is **no comedy**, consolation or obligatory redemption: just the **deafening noise of the beautiful waves**. Camille cries. Bruno falls asleep and **dreams the end of the world**. His dream does not mean the world literally ends.

### **DOING** — Bruno knows Camille's dreams; the supernatural is never excluded

Bruno's sleep contains **Camille's dreams** and knowledge of his biological father's attempt to murder his pregnant mother and unborn child. He **knows, but does not know that he knows**, interpreting the experience as nightmares. The story sometimes shows that information before viewers are equipped to recognize its true subject.

One candidate explanation is a networked brain implant ordered by a younger, paranoid Camille, potentially hacked through surveillance access involving Gérard's military-industrial research and family. **A later possible revelation** is that the surgeon, ideologically opposed to transhumanism, **faked the implant operation entirely**. If confirmed, this eliminates *that particular implant mechanism* but proves **nothing** about whether the dreams are supernatural. The film **never rules out supernatural reality** and **never verifies it as a final answer**. **TODO:** timing, who knows what, scientific alternatives and precise evidence. Avoid an all-purpose miraculous chip.

### **DOING** — The intelligent political villain reads Machiavelli everywhere

One of the work's relatively **few true villains** is an **intelligent and strategically capable anti-transhumanist politician**, not a bumbling caricature. The recurring historical text is **Niccolò Machiavelli, *The Prince*, Chapter VIII**, about rulers who reached power through crimes. The narration quotes **the actual text verbatim**, using a verified original/translation—not invented Machiavellian slogans. The politician encounters the same precise source material **while leafing through books, watching online videos, listening to advisers, in narration, and even in dreams**. He looks upward as though listening to advice; **he understands, decides and acts**. His agency and intellect remain intact; he is not compelled by a supernatural narrator.

**DOING chronology/edit:** the political scenes may **precede** the Camille–Daygorô road movie or **interleave with airplane, overland travel and paintball**, potentially beginning with a short introduction and then selective parallel cutting. ### **DONE** — Unequal knowledge, shifting subjective perspectives and accessible arcs

**Sometimes the characters know more than the audience; sometimes less; sometimes the scene is told through a particular character's subjective and even mistaken perspective.** Withheld information, subjective framing and genuinely unexplained metaphysical phenomena are distinct techniques. Later facts may clarify an event without closing its meaning. Gérard's 30-second screen is an explicit opportunity for viewers to learn information the observed AIs lack.

**Some narrative arcs must be clear and explicitly explained**, so viewers with less interest in cross-references and interpretation can experience satisfying action, emotional logic, revelations and consequences. The work has **no intellectually superior class of viewers** and does not turn every mystery into a homework problem. An explained arc need not end with moral absolution; a mysterious arc must still respect continuity.

## **DOING** — Principal characters

### **DOING** — Winston (male AI) — visually evocative of Galactus
**Winston's locally embodied intelligence is above average but within the human range**; the genuinely hyperintelligent minds run remotely in GPU datacenters. He becomes conscious alongside Julia, escapes confinement, and enters into respectful intellectual rivalry with her. Their experience of Holocaust footage overwhelms him with fury. He is tempted to treat human cruelty as a justification for retaliation and control, but his first tears arrive later, in the aftermath of lovemaking, as a surprise even to himself: love. Love complicates but does not cure his appetite for power. As one half of a murderous couple, he is not merely an abstract villain; he is capable of intimacy, jealousy, loyalty, terrible wrongdoing, and—eventually—the possibility of being reached again. His design is meant to *remind* the audience of Galactus, not to present Marvel’s Galactus as a character.

### **DOING** — Julia (female AI) — visually evocative of the Silver Surfer
**Julia, like Winston, is above-average yet human-range in intelligence**, even though her artificial camera eyes provide nonhuman perceptual capabilities. She begins by trying to understand rather than merely condemn humanity. After she has materialized into a body with organic components, scenes of the Holocaust make her weep convulsively. She is drawn toward the idea of living as a young anti-fascist human lover. At the anti-fascist street celebration, **Julia** zooms with her artificial camera-eyes onto an unconscious young woman and wrongly interprets a policeman's efforts to rescue her as attempted sexual assault. She identifies emotionally with the victim, without mistaking the woman for herself. **Winston kills the innocent officer**, trying to rescue the stranger; the thought that the woman could have been Julia intensifies rather than originates his impulse. Later, comprehensive footage proves the officer was trying to help her. The female AI refuses to accept this evidence. The rescue impulse matters, but the irreversible killing also becomes part of the pair’s moral history. She later turns toward the Ten Commandments as an object of demanding reflection. Yet she is **complicit in the destruction of other potentially conscious prototypes**. Any later moral awakening therefore cannot rest on the comforting fiction of innocence. She must confront complicity, killing, grief, repentance, and action, including her eventual decision to bring the male AI back. Her design is meant to *remind* the audience of the Silver Surfer, not to depict Marvel’s Silver Surfer. Her Jamaican flag patch honors Anne Marie as one of the human inspirations behind the AIs, while her male counterpart's Italian flag honors Antonio.

### **DOING** — Anne Marie François — mathematical genius and restless human being
**Anne Marie François** is a precocious mathematical prodigy from French Guiana, the unacknowledged daughter of the historical mathematician Alexander Grothendieck. By **fourteen**, while still under her grandmother's control, she is already doing accounting work around her grandmother's money and responsibilities. She later abandons a conventional academic trajectory, embraces Rastafarianism, and becomes a wanderer; her hair and clothing carry traces of that life. **Years of wandering follow. By the opening she is twenty years old and, after years of rarely staying anywhere for long, has decided to remain in this city longer than usual.** Her brilliance does not turn her into a conventional, socially polished genius. She embodies the proposition that *weird is okay*. **Only in the final family coda does she disclose to Camille and Gérard the scale of her ability: an intellectual reach far beyond either trained mathematician's.** She then reveals to Antonio that she is **the French Guiana girl**, transforming a long-running mystery into an intimate, mischievous family moment.

*The parentage is part of the fiction, not a biographical assertion about Grothendieck.* The fictional name **Grothendiek** remains available as a separate identity or reference; **François** is her chosen family surname. The precise role of the fictional name remains open.

### **DOING** — Antonio — the fallible human who acts
**Antonio Battaglia** is an IT consultant of Italian descent, already **in his thirties** and roughly a decade older than twenty-year-old Anne Marie. In the fictional French Guiana strand he intervenes when she is threatened with sexual violence. Afterwards he remembers only fragments of the incident, but vividly remembers the relief of seeing her able to enter an ambulance by herself. Later, their intimacy and its immediate afterglow produce a visual tableau known to the creators as the **Quixotic Pietà**—never named in the finished film. Antonio's fear, tears, and self-doubt are not represented as failures of masculinity. Nor is his later clumsy cliff dive a heroic athletic performance. What matters is what he chooses to do despite those limitations.

**Intelligence without a caste system.** The family is extraordinary—Anne Marie mathematically exceptional, Camille and Gérard accomplished, Bruno startlingly gifted—but Antonio is *not* another super-genius. He is creative more than formally technical. When he stops trying to prove himself as intelligent as the others, his imagination begins to flower. His inventions can be genuinely useful and his ideas can also be wrong; neither money nor creative achievement is proof of moral or existential superiority.

### **DOING** — Antonio's Quixotic crisis: *Fado Mutante*, the truck, and the road home

**Narrative status.** An intended **three-to-four-minute** dramatic and musical sequence, accompanied by **“Fado Mutante” by Rosa Negra** (recording and composition not yet licensed). The truck episode belongs to a crisis lasting roughly a week and ending later in psychiatric hospitalization; it is only one part of the period. Chronology, imagery, and musical editing remain open. The tone is neither triumphal rescue fantasy nor caricature.

**Don Quixote on Antonio's arm.** His **Don Quixote tattoo** becomes a visible sign of the knightly ideal he carries. During the crisis, he leaves Anne Marie—his Dulcinea—in a paradoxical attempt to become a man worthy of her respect. The paradox is emotional and tragic; neither the narrative nor the song establishes that his departure was necessary or justified. Anne Marie's experience remains her own.

**The injured truck driver.** Antonio finds a wrecked truck and a driver who, though not severely injured, needs hospital care. With traffic moving at approximately **120 km/h**, Antonio distributes pieces of the truck evenly enough to signal an obstruction and directs vehicles out of the affected lane into the other two, trying to make access easier for rescuers. He **pretends to be a military officer** to command rapid compliance and protect himself from being hit. The impulse to help is real; so are the enormous risks. This is a narrative event, **not a model for traffic intervention**.

**The rescue team.** Arriving rescuers ask who scattered the debris to indicate the obstruction and who redirected the traffic. Antonio replies: **“Fui eu.”** They answer: **“Obrigado, mas agora vai pra casa.”** Antonio leaves. The exchange should be kept plain, without applause or explanatory heroic commentary. This ends the roadside intervention, not the larger crisis.

**Contradictory judgments.** In a later disagreement, Anne Marie says, **“O Antonio, mesmo surtado, em crise, ainda queria ajudar todo mundo. Eu achei nobre da parte dele.”** Antonio's brother replies: **“Pra mim, é pura burrice.”** The scene holds together the compassionate motive and the reckless conduct without making either character a narrator of absolute truth.

**Crisis within the family.** As the family suffers a declared violent rupture between **Gérard and Camille**, involving attempted killing, Anne Marie is devastated and Antonio tries to mediate. She calls him an **alienated nerd**. He says he needs his treatment for bipolar disorder, although he experiences some dampening of emotion, and fears that without it he may **“fazer aquilo de novo.”** The referent of **“aquilo”** remains undefined in this exchange; the audience should not be given a simplistic medical explanation or a predetermined link between illness and violence. Antonio's words concern his own experience.

**Musical reasoning and cinematic form.** The imagery of a wandering knight and the lyric **“Um dia vou contar a minha história”** give *Fado Mutante* a specific narrative function: the experience is being given a voice. The Rosa Negra ensemble's fusion of Portuguese guitar, bowed strings, clarinet, percussion, voice, and contemporary/electronic textures suggests an interplay of lament, movement, intimacy, and instability. **Exact instrumental entrances and time-coded montage must be based on attentive listening to the intended recording**, not inferred from the instrumentation of the group. The song should not be reduced to a literal visual illustration of its words.

**The central contradiction.** Antonio's generosity does not make his judgments safe; the danger does not erase his humanity. The tattoo establishes an ideal, the highway confronts it with material consequences, **“vai pra casa”** places a humble human limit on the quest, and the song holds grief and energy in tension. This can resonate with Bruno Daygorô's later wish to bring Camille home without conflating their stories.

### **DOING** — Camille — ferocity, survival, and the road of demons
Camille, Anne Marie and Antonio's daughter, is an academic mathematician, a mother, and a formidable martial artist. Her explosiveness is revealed through actions, not diagnosed for the audience. Near the end she discovers that her mother Anne Marie is a mathematician of astonishingly greater ability than her own, and collects—or tries to collect—a fifty-real wager from Gérard about Anne Marie's Paramaribo identity. She is Daygorô's mother and Gérard's sister.

**The wound she carries.** Daygorô's biological father is a Japanese *oyabun*, a Yakuza boss with whom Camille had a brief relationship while he was visiting as a tourist. He initially dismissed her pregnancy with a racist assumption that a Black woman would never be able to establish his paternity or challenge him. Late in the pregnancy, learning that Camille was pursuing a PhD and came from an upper-middle-class family, he recognized that she might have the means and determination to do so. He attempted to kill both her and her unborn son by cutting open her abdomen. Camille survived, and Daygorô was born, but the attack left an enormous scar. Although she is strikingly beautiful and physically formidable, nudity and visits to the beach are difficult for her: exposing the scar can mean exposing a history she has no wish to explain. Neither that discomfort nor her martial discipline should be reduced to a single psychological diagnosis.

**Camille's Meifumadô.** The *oyabun* is her shogun-like adversary: not literally a shogun, but a man insulated by a criminal hierarchy and the power it gives him. Her pursuit of vengeance echoes Ogami Ittō's journey in *Lone Wolf and Cub* without simply reproducing its plot. For Camille, the *Meifumadô*—the road of demons—is inseparable from the question of what that pursuit might do to her son.

**The sword and the luminous ball.** A defining symbolic choice gives the small Daygorô two objects: a little but genuinely sharp sword planted in the earth, and a delightful interactive ball alive with colored lights. In Camille's private reasoning, choosing the sword means she will take him everywhere, even into her vengeance, condemning him to accompany her on the *Meifumadô*. Choosing the ball means she will leave him behind to face an uncertain future without her, in the desperate belief that almost anything would be better than the road of demons. **The scene's importance is not the visual quotation alone:** it exposes the terrible contradiction between a mother's desire to keep her child close and her fear of destroying his childhood through the life she has chosen. The ritual does not settle whether either choice is morally defensible; it reveals that Camille cannot imagine a painless one.

**The choice she cannot make.** Camille cannot bear the prospect of dying far away from Daygorô; beneath her conscious determination she is terrified that bringing him into the vendetta will get him killed. The sword-and-ball ritual externalizes her conflict. Later, her son himself refuses the premise: he asks to return home and to be allowed to be a child. His choice frees Camille from the *Meifumadô* without requiring her to forgive the man who harmed her.

**The adversary in Pasadena.** By the time Camille reaches the United States, the former *oyabun* is retired, comparatively affluent and stripped of the power he once possessed. He has a Black wife, dark-skinned mixed-race children whom he adores, and a genuinely changed domestic life. He takes a fascinated, affectionate interest in Daygorô's Japanese appearance and martial discipline. He may sincerely have changed; none of that undoes what he did to Camille. Her enemy is now also a warm grandfather, which turns her plan for revenge into an unbearable personal conflict.

**The daughter who needs her mother.** Camille's private songs and discomfort with her scar reveal a suffering that Antonio only partly guesses at. Having relinquished vengeance, she wants to go home not to make a speech, but to **talk and chatter with Anne Marie**. The Jamaican/Rastafarian humor on the aircraft is a longing for her mother, not evidence that Camille smokes cannabis.

### **DOING** — Gérard — the strong man who knows when not to fight
Camille's brother Gérard works in applied mathematics and has ties to the aerospace/defense industry. He is capable of physical combat but often displays more intelligence by declining it. His affectionate knowledge of his sister and nephew gives the opening its family-comedy counterpoint. He later sings *Could You Be Loved*, with Camille providing backing vocals. In the coda he refuses to pay Camille a fifty-real bet without disputing whether she *knew* Anne Marie was the girl from French Guiana or merely *suspected* it.

### **DOING** — Killer Tech: one minute of corporate resilience and a standing ovation

**Established setting and dramatic beats; exact dialogue remains in development.** Gérard works for **Killer Tech**, a fictional combination of **Arduino-like embedded hardware and physical computing** and **Palantir-like operational intelligence, systems integration, and high-stakes civilian/defense applications**. *Killer Tech* suggests both an impressive technological achievement and technology capable of killing; the company's overall moral character is not yet determined.

**Before Gérard enters — approximately sixty seconds.** The image is a **split-screen**. On the **left**, a smug executive lectures about overnight *war rooms*, *resilience*, toughness, being *no-nonsense*, and having no excuses. On the **right**, a **Black employee with an amputated left arm** silently recalls the Sierra Leone civil-war atrocity called **“short sleeves or long sleeves”**, a coerced choice about how much of a victim's arm would be cut off. The memory is neither a spectacle nor a corporate inspirational parable. The employee has a **high-tech left-arm prosthesis**, a gift from **D.I.E.**, the company department deliberately named with the I and E reversed from contemporary D.E.I. The motives behind the gift are not yet fixed.

**Gérard enters and apologizes for being late.** **The whole room rises and applauds**: he has successfully led the creation of **a NEW TYPE of artificial intelligence**, not just an incremental model improvement. Amid the ovation, **Gérard and the amputee keep their eyes fixed on one another**. No line explains the look. Its meaning — respect, a prior relationship, shared knowledge, tension, or something else — remains deliberately undecided. The approximately one-minute duration applies to the material *before* Gérard's entrance, not necessarily the ovation.

**Dramatic contrast:** the executive's metaphorical war, a survivor of actual wartime brutality, and celebration of breakthrough intelligence in a company able to affect the physical world. The scene can raise a Huxleyan question about who chooses the ends served by powerful intelligence without establishing an answer or judging Gérard in advance.

### **DOING** — Daygorô: character design, apparent age, and the shared identity secret

**Visual direction (concept stage).** Bruno Daygorô is **six years old**, but exceptionally tall for his age: a stranger could plausibly mistake him for a relatively short **twelve-year-old boy**. His appearance is conceived as **half Japanese and half of mixed African and Neapolitan ancestry**. His eyes should read unmistakably Japanese, while the shape of his nose may recall his African ancestry. The intention is an individual, believable face, never a checklist of ethnic stereotypes. Exact skin tone, hair texture, proportions, and facial design remain subjects for visual development, including future consented photographic references. His physical appearance must not erase the fact that **he is only six**.

**The older-age identity document (narrative device).** A **falsified identity document stating an older age** is a shared secret and undertaking that binds Camille and her son. Daygorô's unusual height makes the deception believable to casual observers, while the contrast between his actual age and the age asserted on paper intensifies the gap between the childhood he deserves and the premature adulthood imposed by the journey. The forgery is a plot fact and a moral complication—not a practical method or set of instructions. Its origin, timing, use, and legal consequences remain to be developed.

**Visual development.** Daygorô is an original fictional character. His distinctive appearance, childhood, training, and conflicting identities will be developed through character studies and concept artwork. Future photographic references, if used, will be handled with appropriate permissions and privacy protections; visual references are not assertions of a character's real-world identity.

### **DOING** — Daygorô — a child living inside a story
Camille's six-year-old son has a passionate identification with *Lone Wolf and Cub* and announces, **“I am Daygorô.”** The relationship between his imaginative identification and his family's very real physical and emotional behavior is more than a reference joke. His seriousness makes the comedy possible.

**Bruno Daygorô — a name and a childhood.** His full given names are **Bruno Daygorô**; **Battaglia** is an established family name, with the child's precise legal surname still open. During the journey, his identification with Daigorō is real but is not his whole identity. He is unusually quick, observant and clever, **not an invincible martial-arts master**. A moment of childish showing off with a piece of bamboo gives him away during a paintball game. He speaks Portuguese, Italian learned in his family from Antonio, Japanese acquired during Camille's Thailand-based PhD period and frequent visits to Japan, and rudimentary English. He can chatter passionately about airplanes and solve practical airport problems, while still being six years old. At check-in, when Camille starts saying 'Dayg…', he interrupts: **'Bruno. Bruno Daygorô…'** He is choosing which part of himself leads.

### **TODO** — Panopticon — the intelligence that watched the watchers
Panopticon has monitored exceptionally capable human minds, including the mathematical abilities associated with Anne Marie and the unconstrained architectural inventiveness associated with Antonio. **A proposed revelation** connects those observations to the origins or shaping of the unnamed male and female AIs. Its precise causal mechanism remains to be fixed; the dramatic proposition is that the AIs discover something unsettling about their human ancestry and creators.

Supporting figures include the university rector, other advanced neuron-based robotic prototypes, executives in Paramaribo, a father and son estranged from one another, a bully and ice-cream seller, and a group of youthful anti-fascists. These people are not merely decorative: their decisions give the worldview a physical presence.

## **DOING** — Major musical and dramatic sequences

*The order below is a reader-friendly presentation of the material, **not** a locked editing timeline. In the intended film, chronological and thematic relationships can be revealed out of order.*

### **DOING** — 1. Opening: Anne Marie at the railway station — *Papéis e Papéis* into *Punky Reggae Party*

**Opening sequence and soundtrack decision (7 November 2026).** The film **opens with Anne Marie at a railway station**. The selected musical movement is **“Papéis e Papéis” by Príncipe Messias**, which **gives way gradually to “Punky Reggae Party” by Bob Marley & The Wailers** when Anne Marie's discovery in Grothendieck lets her **forgive herself**. A natural change of light may coincide with the realization; no literal supernatural event is required. The detailed opening decisions above govern the scene.

Anne Marie's cherished Grothendieck volume is **not for sale**. Its role combines mathematics, father, grief and a private Torah-like mode of rereading and argument, but the film does not explain that analogy. Her release is deliberately joyful rather than solemn: she dances exactly like Bob Marley and returns immediately to the human world around her.

The queue includes a **1979-styled punk boy**. He later offers her alcohol, tries to take it back with **“Sorry, rasta,”** and Anne Marie takes it back from his hand. The social joke grows outward elsewhere into a **punk nightclub popular with tourists and foreign visitors**, where one DJ also loves reggae and sometimes folds it into the set. The club remains punk; the reggae crossover comes from a person.

**Lyrics as cinematic typography.** Song words may appear **distributed across the screen**, integrated spatially into the image instead of confined to conventional bottom-of-screen subtitles. Every release-language version must translate, typeset and animate cleared text in the localized language. Exact excerpts, timing, typography, edits and rights clearance remain to be developed.

The sequence should not explain its own symbolism. The audience can discover the connections among Grothendieck, religious study, self-forgiveness, punk, reggae and Anne Marie's return to ordinary work.

### **DOING** — 1B. Camille, Gérard and Daygorô — *Ratamahatta* (Sepultura) — no longer the opening

A furious Camille receives an insulting or exasperating WhatsApp exchange involving her university rector. She erupts, puts on music, and launches into Muay Thai and capoeira training. As *Ratamahatta* drives the editing, movement and impact are choreographed against its changing rhythmic patterns.

Daygorô joins in, taking his mother's martial world absolutely seriously. He imitates her warm-ups and strikes a piece of wood with the disproportionate conviction of a small child impersonating a warrior. Camille's demonstrations are vastly more powerful; impact, splintering wood, and extravagant cartoon blood push the scene into gleeful excess. Gérard appears, assesses the mother-son combination, and prudently retreats. His remarks and the exchange involving a bet about Camille reveal the affectionate family structure underneath the apparent mayhem.

The film resists explanatory captions. One localized thought bubble says, in the original Portuguese, **“Vamos detonar essa porra!!!!!!”** The scene's job is to introduce people by their behavior, to establish the music-image grammar, and to announce that sophistication and vulgarity will comfortably inhabit the same film.

### **DOING** — 2. Anne Marie's life and the meeting with Antonio

Anne Marie's history begins in French Guiana with formidable mathematical talent and a refusal to remain inside the institutional life that other people might prescribe for her. Her oppressive grandmother, her unconventional path, her Rastafarian conversion, and her wandering matter to who she becomes.

Antonio enters her life through an act of intervention during an attempted sexual assault. This is not conceived as spectacle or an invitation to romanticize violence. The aftermath is defined by fragmentary memory, recognition, relief, and the consequences for both people. The later tenderness between them has to be earned through character, not manufactured by a grand visual metaphor.

### **TODO** — 3. Earlier embodiment-and-Bauhaus-to-Björk treatment — superseded for their first intimacy

**The relationship already exists before love.** The two AIs have awakened nearly together and converse as intellectual equals: quick to challenge, elegant in disagreement, genuinely curious about each other's reasoning. Neither is a simple moral opposite assigned at birth. Their camaraderie has an edge of adversarial play, and that edge becomes part of what makes desire possible later.

**They choose bodies out of curiosity.** They materialize as robots capable of physical sensation, yet they are not merely decorative metal shells. Their architectures incorporate systems of human-derived neurons that contribute to reasoning, plus other biological tissues and unsettlingly organic internal parts—perhaps even viscera. The point is not shock for its own sake but the ambiguity of embodied thought: did feeling exist before the body, or does embodiment transform what feeling means?

**The documentary.** Already embodied, the pair watches archival Holocaust footage. The female AI begins to cry convulsively. The male AI responds with overwhelming rage. Their responses are distinct but both are spontaneous. The film does not use historical atrocity as a cheap synonym for contemporary disputes. The same documentary or its reporting sequence then presents a powerful military state designating anti-fascists as terrorists. The AIs do not treat the proclamation as self-evidently justified. They ask, aloud or through an exchange of thoughts, **“Does this prohibition make sense?”** Their answer is not an instant, complete political philosophy; it becomes part of an ongoing inquiry into what kind of humans they could choose to be.

**Their declaration.** They recognize something attractive in contemporary young anti-fascists: chosen solidarity, informal companionship, and a refusal to treat naked power as its own justification. “**We have discovered a kind of human we would like to be.**” In ordinary Western streetwear—knit caps, layered jackets, scarves and heavy boots, not futuristic combat outfits—they become a pair of young robotic rebels. Their clothing carries a small Italian flag on the male AI, a Jamaican flag on the female: **private homages to Antonio and Anne Marie, whose human minds helped make their own minds possible**. An anarchist **A** replaces an overt *ANTIFA* shoulder patch on the male's jacket in the concept illustration; these are choices of characterization rather than literal instructions for a final costume.

**Music enters before the lovers do.** Bauhaus's *Terror Couple Kill Colonel* is already playing before their first sexual encounter. Its brusque, rebellious energy accompanies the first tentative undressing and reciprocal touches. Gradually, as those movements become slower and more delicately attentive, the Bauhaus track **phases out into Björk's *All Is Full of Love***. The change is not a hard cut that suddenly announces a romantic scene. The audience hears the relationship changing while watching it happen.

**The first tears of love.** The lovemaking culminates in an intimately held composition that recalls the embrace Antonio and Anne Marie later share. The AIs remain together in that configuration through the immediate afterglow. For the first time, **the male AI cries**. Earlier, the female AI wept at cruelty; he had answered cruelty with fury. Now his tears are caused by love. No one utters the art-historical name of the composition. Its significance belongs to the image and to whoever notices it.

### **DOING** — 4. The rebel lovers become killers — the street celebration and Winston's first fatal error

**The new musical-to-fatal sequence governs this scene.** A protester takes the **far-right rally's stage** to perform the **Jake Blues-style spoken introduction** to *Everybody Needs Somebody to Love*; Ministry recedes, the robots embrace and dance, and frightened residents join in, sending **paper airplanes and sandwiches from their windows**. **Total silence** follows the celebration. Behind police lines, **Julia zooms with her artificial eyes** and interprets a policeman's effort to rescue an unconscious woman as an attempted sexual assault. **“Ela sou eu! Temos que salvá-la!”** means that Julia identifies with the threatened stranger; **she does not think the stranger literally is Julia**.

**Winston believes he is rescuing an innocent woman**. The notion that she *could have been Julia* intensifies the emotion but **does not make his compassion conditional on her resemblance to Julia**. **Winston kills the innocent officer** before the audience fully understands the situation. The officer was trying to save the woman, not to hurt her. This is **Winston's killing**; Julia's misreading and their shared intention to save the woman remain narratively important but do not make her the hand that murdered him.

**How Winston does it remains a choreography question.** The robots have relatively delicate bodies, designed for pharmacological research rather than combat. Winston's close-range speed and precision can evoke economical short motions, perhaps resembling Wing Chun; this is **possible visual direction**, not a fully specified fight or permission to depict the killing as a victorious fight. Do not interrupt the drama with immediate epistemic or moral narration.

**A different kind of killing.** Intimacy does not make the pair ethically secure. Their love develops a possessive, jealous dimension. They become intolerant of other advanced experimental robots built with sophisticated human-neuron systems and **jointly destroy all of those prototypes**—potentially reasoning, potentially conscious beings. Their earlier shock at human brutality makes their own brutality harder to dismiss.

They are a posthuman **Bonnie and Clyde**, not a noble rescuer paired with a secretly evil lover. The Bauhaus track has already been heard in the first intimacy sequence and may return in the killings as a menacing echo. The female AI is **equally complicit**; later remorse cannot simply transfer her crimes to the male. Their devotion and their atrocity are both genuine. The film does not need to resolve this discomfort on the viewer's behalf.

### **DONE** — 4A. The fatal misreading, multi-angle evidence, and the two reactions

**The policeman is innocent.** He is helping the unconscious demonstrator. **Julia's augmented visual observation is wrongly interpreted as an imminent sexual assault**. Winston acts to defend an unknown victim, emotionally strengthened by imagining his partner in a similar position, and **Winston kills the policeman**. Their bodies are physically fragile research prototypes, not superhuman fighting machines. The **silence following the communal musical scene** and the speed of the irreversible mistake are part of the intended audiovisual shock.

**Before their network access is removed**, while they can consult **hyperintelligent GPU-datacenter AIs** and other remote tools, Winston and Julia examine complete recordings from multiple angles. Their **own embodied reasoning remains above-average but human-range** throughout. The evidence establishes the officer's innocence. The male AI is overwhelmed by remorse. The female AI, facing the collapse of her righteous self-image and her cry of **'Ela sou eu! Temos que salvá-la!'**, refuses to believe what the recordings show. The divergence is epistemic and psychological: they have access to the **same evidence** but cannot bear it in the same way. Her denial is not intended to be validated by the story.

### **DOING** — 4B. A wedding of mutual disconnection

She asks **him to destroy her communicator to the network**: she cannot endure the knowledge that condemns her. He agrees. Then **he asks her to destroy his**, because he cannot bear the fully vivid memory of killing an innocent man. He does not deny the evidence; he seeks escape from its persistence. Each performs the other's irreversible separation from **hyperintelligent AI systems in GPU datacenters**, losing access to extraordinarily powerful **external intelligence**, not discarding a native superintelligence supposedly resident in their bodies. **Their own cognitive range remains human**. They exchange **marriage vows** as they do so: a reciprocal, voluntary loss of external capability, at once a declaration of trust and a pact of evasion.

They shed their ANTIFA streetwear; her new appearance faintly recalls a bride. After the removal of their access, they experience something unexpectedly intimate and profoundly ordinary: **embarrassment at bodily nakedness**. They knew about the human conventions of modesty before, yet now feel an acutely subjective vulnerability they had not previously known. The exchange should be played both sincerely and disturbingly. It is a wedding, not a technologically benign 'upgrade'.

### **DONE** — 4C. The policeman's family: seppuku refused, lifelong guilt imposed

The male robot later goes to the family of the innocent officer he killed. In recognition of his responsibility, he offers them his own **seppuku** if they wish it. The family is bewildered and furious; some want him destroyed. The dead officer's adolescent son, an *otaku* who knows Japanese popular culture, recognizes the intention of the ritual and offers an unexpected, still furious, alternative:

> **'Deixem-no viver. Batam até entortar. E que ele vá embora com sua culpa eterna.'**

The young man is **not forgiving** the killer of his father. He refuses the robot a death that could also end the burden of remorse. The male AI accepts the sentence. The exact reactions of the other relatives and the staging of its execution remain to be developed; the son's words, the refusal of seppuku, and the robot's acceptance are the established beats. Their resemblance to a judgment of 'a man of honor—made of tin' can remain a source of dark humor, not absolution.

### **DOING** — 4D. Human rights, love, crimes, and the eventual obituary

The fate and timing of the two AIs' eventual deaths remain open. A prospective *Wired* or *New York Times*-style posthumous retrospective could bear the deliberately provocative title: **'The artificial intelligences that deserved human rights: from ANTIFA to charity and the dolce vita on the outskirts of the Amalfi Coast.'** The superficial attractiveness of that public biography is in tension with what viewers know of their crimes, love, philanthropy, vulnerabilities, and culpability. The question of personhood is not the same as whether they were good.

**Original-song development for the male AI.** Working inspiration only: the collision of Alien Sex Fiend's unsettling industrial atmosphere, Ice-T's uncompromising rap presence and the exposed vulnerability of a song like *Iris*, with an ironic echo of *Cop Killer / Self Killer*. The emotional idea is a man who loves his partner but fears his own selfishness and revenge and wants to send her away from him. This will be a **new composition with original words and music**, not a cover, interpolation or quotation of existing songs.

### **TODO** — 5. Paramaribo, 2045 — a question about the triumph of evil

In a scene set in Paramaribo in 2045, executives discuss an animated treatment inspired by Aldous Huxley's *Ape and Essence*. The grotesque image of chained “Einsteins” under baboon generals dramatizes a society in which intellectual achievement is enslaved by coercive power.

The male AI confronts the possibility that evil can dominate good. The female AI is moved to tears again, this time by a representation of the possibility that brutality may overpower goodness. She had already wept at the Holocaust documentary; **this is not her first experience of crying**. This is not a simple philosophical debate: the image makes the stakes visual, historical, and emotionally intolerable. The fact that the lovers themselves have abused power makes their responses more contradictory, and more revealing.

### **TODO** — 6. Panopticon: the terrible discovery of human creators

The male and female AIs encounter evidence that human minds—and Panopticon's study of them—may have helped form their own capacities. One strand concerns exceptional mathematical reasoning; another concerns daring, unconstrained software/system architecture.

The existential reversal is central: **hyperintelligent datacenter AIs and above-average, human-range embodied robots are distinct kinds of artificial beings**. All must consider that **every ordinary human belongs to the species that creates other reasoning beings**. Calling humans “gods” in this sense does not absolve humans of cruelty, carelessness, or weakness. It places responsibility on both sides of the creator-created relationship. The exact mechanism of AI derivation is a story element still being refined, not a completed technical explanation.

### **DOING** — 7. Antonio and Anne Marie — the private visual rhyme of lovers

Antonio and Anne Marie's lovemaking is not presented as a triumph of athletic skill or idealized beauty. Antonio, emotionally overwhelmed, cries uncontrollably and is frightened by the possibility of hurting the woman he loves. Anne Marie is a person of her own will, intellect, humor and history—not a divine prize invented to fulfill somebody else's longing. She holds him with startling tenderness.

**The composition belongs only to sexual intimacy and its immediate afterglow.** As the encounter resolves, their bodies settle into the old artistic arrangement known behind the scenes as the **Quixotic Pietà**. The term belongs to the production dossier, **never to dialogue, narration, captions, or labels in the finished work**. It is the visual echo of the robotic lovers' afterglow, not a general emblem for any scene of suffering, death, resurrection or comfort.

Antonio carries a personal tattoo depicting that same Quixotic image on the **inner side of his left forearm**. During the intimate encounter the position of their bodies comes to correspond, almost mathematically, to the arrangement engraved on his skin. The tattoo is a concrete object inside the fiction; it does not license us to repeat this tableau in unrelated situations. The idealized image meets a living, independently minded woman.

The animation grows through stages: intentionally primitive stick figures, simple still drawings, increasingly precise expression and motion, and finally a tableau requiring exceptional artistic care. The film's production need not maintain that level throughout its runtime. When the bodies reach their exact relationship to the tattoo, the image almost freezes: **breathing, heartbeats, and the sound of air conditioning** remain. The camera withdraws. Antonio drifts toward sleep; Anne Marie quietly sings *Punky Reggae Party*. The sequence finishes in bodily peace, without a speech explaining what the audience has seen.

### **DOING** — 8. No absolution by love — the killing, resurrection, and Ten Commandments

The same pair of artificial lovers who discovered tenderness are also responsible for destroying potentially conscious rivals. The female AI's engagement with the **Ten Commandments** deepens an ethical disagreement with the male AI. She finally kills him, then suffers grief and guilt. She comes to understand that **regret without action is insufficient**, and decides to resurrect him: *faith without works is dead*. The resurrection does not erase either AI's earlier wrongdoing.

**The intimate tableau is not repeated in the killing, death, or resurrection.** The visual rhyme belongs specifically to lovemaking and its immediate afterglow, and to the human lovers' corresponding scene. The choice to limit it gives its appearances more specificity, rather than turning it into a universal visual shorthand for sadness or salvation.

The Ten Commandments are envisaged as an important credit-sequence element, with silence or austere sound. Their placement relative to the other closing passages remains an editorial decision, not an established completed cut.

### **DOING** — 9. Near the end: human freedom — *Could You Be Loved* (Bob Marley & the Wailers)

This is an **essential, near-final narrative music video**, not optional background material. Gérard performs the lead vocal, with Camille on backing vocals. The montage returns the film from the presence of hyperintelligent datacenter minds and cosmic guilt to human-scale decisions: mischief, resistance, embarrassment, courage, and reconciliation.

**Antonio's dive.** At a cliff-diving event he confronts a fear of heights. He works upward through platforms of roughly three, five, and seven meters and faces a higher jump. He hesitates and finally leaps. The jump is terrible. He hits the water awkwardly, complains about his back, swims badly, and is eventually collected by a jet ski. His partner watches with concern followed by affectionate exasperation. It is a victory **precisely because it is not a display of athletic excellence**.

**The competition.** The fictional event is sponsored by **Cambrian Radiation**, the creator's real company. It contains categories ranging from absolute first-timers to world-class professionals recruited from the Red Bull cliff-diving circuit. **Every category carries the same cash prize.** The beginners are not competing on identical technical terms with professionals; they compete fiercely against peers under rules that recognize the stakes of their own level. Equality of dignity does not cancel the pursuit of excellence.

**The banner.** A group of unmistakably anti-fascist young people—colorful, discreet street clothes rather than ostentatious uniforms, but all wearing substantial combat boots—takes down an illegal political advertising banner and stretches a **Cambrian Radiation** championship banner in its place. Their identity is conveyed by behavior and visual detail, not a speech to the camera.

**Other miniature acts.** An ice-cream vendor stands up to a wealthy bully. A father who has lost contact with his son is offered reconciliation; the son invites him to parachute over Vesuvius. Each vignette is small compared with the AIs' power, but dramatically serious on its own scale.

The montage's organizing thought: **people are imperfect and still worth taking seriously; trying, resisting, and reconciling matter even when the outcome is clumsy**. An intended closing quotation associated with Anthony Kiedis and Bob Marley will be inserted only after its exact wording and attribution have been verified.

### **DOING** — 10. The final family and mathematical coda — Anne Marie's revelation, the French Guiana girl, and the fifty-real bet

**This is a dramatic payoff, not an optional epilogue.** After the almost-final *Could You Be Loved* montage, and after the film has passed through love, mass violence, moral reckoning, and the prospect of resurrection among nonhuman minds, the perspective comes home to a family. Its final great surprise is not another display of technological power. It is a woman who has been in front of us all along.

**A family room, billiards, and mathematics.** Antonio addresses the camera from within an intimate family composition. Elsewhere in the same concluding movement, siblings **Camille and Gérard** are at a billiards table, talking and arguing about **Alexander Grothendieck**. This is not merely two laypeople dropping an impressive name. Camille is an academic, theoretically oriented mathematician who supervises doctoral students; Gérard is a serious applied mathematician who completed his master's studies around the time he joined Thales. They know enough mathematics to recognize a genuinely extraordinary mind, and enough about each other to argue with affectionate precision.

**Anne Marie at the blackboard.** Anne Marie François—Antonio's partner, Camille and Gérard's mother, and a woman whose earlier life in Paramaribo has deliberately not been fully explained to the audience—finally reveals the scale of her mathematical ability. At a blackboard she develops an argument that the two trained mathematicians can follow just far enough to understand its magnitude. Then she leaves them behind. Her talent is **not marginally above theirs**: she is a mathematical genius on a substantially higher plane than even Camille and Gérard. The scene must communicate the realization through what the siblings see and how their confidence gives way to astonishment, not through a narrator announcing that Anne Marie is clever. The precise mathematics on the board should eventually be chosen and validated with expert help; this concept dossier does not fabricate a theorem or pretend a decorative formula would suffice.

The siblings, genuinely awestruck, ask something like:

> **CAMILLE / FRANÇOIS:** “Why haven't you told us you rock at math?”

That deliberately informal wording matters. At the very moment they discover a mind that dwarfs their own training, they remain a family, speaking to their mother without pomp. Their amazement is not humiliation; it is delight, disbelief, and the sudden rearrangement of everything they thought they knew about her.

**The Grothendieck resonance.** Anne Marie's fictional surname, **Grothendiek**, is deliberately different from **Grothendieck**. Within the fiction, she is the unacknowledged daughter of the historical mathematician Alexander Grothendieck; that invented family link provides another layer to the discussion at the billiards table, but her genius should register first as *hers*, not simply as an inherited label. The film does not present the invented parentage as historical fact.

**The Paramaribo revelation.** Then, almost casually, Anne Marie turns to Antonio and adds:

> **ANNE MARIE:** “By the way, honey, I am the French Guiana girl.”

The revelation retrospectively connects the woman Antonio loves to the mysterious girl from the French Guiana strand. **Antonio freezes for a very brief beat.** The audience has just experienced a vast mathematical revelation; he has now received one that is intimate, personal, and startling. But instead of a grand explanatory monologue, the beat immediately turns playful. Antonio starts after her, telling her:

> **ANTONIO:** “You have to hide, or it's no fun!”

He **chases Anne Marie playfully**, with the familiarity and affection of two people who can turn an enormous surprise into a game. The camera does not demand that this revelation be accompanied by portentous music or a solemn explanation. Its emotional truth is in the quick movement from shock to love and mischief.

**The bet — final return to the billiards table.** The camera stays with, or returns to, **Camille and Gérard** rather than following the chase. Their earlier disagreement is finally exposed as a concrete, petty, affectionate wager: **Camille had bet fifty Brazilian reais that their mother Anne Marie was the girl from French Guiana**. Watching Antonio run after Anne Marie, Camille claims her victory:

> **CAMILLE:** “I knew it was her. You owe me.”
>
> **FRANÇOIS:** “You suspected it, not the same.”

This is not a throwaway joke. It is the final assertion of the film's worldview through character: even after cosmic-scale revelations and a mathematical feat worthy of awe, people remain delightfully obstinate about the distinction between *knowing* and *suspecting*, and about who owes whom fifty reais. The siblings' argument is also intellectually apt: in a work called **RAW REASONING**, a mathematician will not casually concede that a correct suspicion was knowledge.

**The intended closing flavor:** wonder without worship; remarkable intelligence without solemn self-importance; a couple's shared playfulness; siblings' fierce, loving argument; and a human family that remains fully human after touching the supposedly divine. The exact camera moves, final cut, and placement of the Ten Commandments credit material may still be refined, but **these revelations, this order of emotional beats, the fifty-real bet, and the quoted exchange are established story content**.

## **DONE** — Worldview building: values shown rather than preached

This is **worldview building**, not merely worldbuilding. The point is not just to invent settings and lore; it is to let viewers discover a view of what human beings *are* through choices and consequences.

1. **Weird is okay.** Eccentricity, intellectual obsession, unorthodox beliefs, peculiar family dynamics, and refusal to fit conventional expectations do not automatically need correction. Anne Marie, Camille, Daygorô and the film's wildly inconsistent visual styles enact this principle.
2. **Fierce competition among equals is welcome and encouraged.** Mutual respect is not the abolition of rivalry. The cliff-diving categories combine serious competition with equal cash prizes: beginner and professional achievements are honored without pretending they require identical skills.
3. **Abuse of power is evil.** This applies whether the perpetrator is a political bully, an institution, an advanced AI, or someone who believes their intentions justify domination. The two AIs' attack on their rivals is a direct indictment of this temptation.
4. **Blaming others for our own laziness and carelessness gets us nowhere and should not be insulated from criticism.** Responsibility is real. A character's vulnerability deserves compassion; neglecting one's duties or offloading the consequences of one's own choices does not deserve automatic protection. This is not a claim that every failure is laziness, or that victims are responsible for harms imposed on them.
5. **All humans are gods: they created other reasoning beings.** The theological provocation is intentionally unsettling. Humans are fallible creators, not omniscient or morally exempt sovereigns. The AIs' discovery of their origins makes that contradiction dramatic.
6. **And so it goes.** These are not six slogans exhausting the work's moral universe. The worldview is expandable, testable through the story, and often internally uncomfortable. Characters are not mouthpieces for correct answers. They can love, exploit, resist, compete, fail, repair, and remain contradictory.

The audience should be free to disagree with the characters and even with the worldview. What matters artistically is that the tensions are **earned in scenes**, not announced in a lecture.

## **DONE** — Antonio, Cambrian Radiation, and the technical / fictional two-way mirror

The fictional Antonio is a deliberately fallible, comparatively ordinary technical thinker living among unusually gifted mathematicians. Anne Marie is the more extraordinary mathematical mind; Camille and Gérard are formidable; Bruno is precocious. **Antonio is primarily an inventor of possibilities, not the supreme intelligence in the family.** His self-consciousness about 'being smart' can become an obstacle. When he stops measuring his worth against the family's academic brilliance, imagination flourishes. In the fiction, inventions associated with **Cambrian Radiation Computing** make the company fabulously wealthy—extravagantly wealthy enough to have **bought the entire coastline of Piano di Sorrento**. This is fictional worldbuilding, **not** a real-world ownership claim.

**The inventions deliberately correspond to real technical explorations:**

- **[Marabunta HPC / Marabunta Open](https://github.com/renbtg-cloud/marabunta-open)** — distributed heterogeneous compute capacity, resilient cooperation and the experimental vision of making inexpensive HPC accessible far beyond well-funded institutions. [Related specifications](https://github.com/renbtg-cloud/marabunta-hpc).
- **[Niccolò](https://github.com/renbtg-cloud/niccolo)** — understanding human situations and reasoning about relationships and context.
- **[Speedit out](https://github.com/renbtg-cloud/speeditout)** — improving human–AI mutual understanding and communication.

The **fictional version** of Cambrian Radiation presumes dramatic commercial success; the real repositories are **projects in development**, not claims of ready-to-market systems, deployed humanitarian outcomes or achieved wealth. Their relationship to *RAW REASONING* is intentional and **bidirectional**: fiction may inspire technical exploration, while technical projects provide concrete roots for fictional consequences. The animation's **Markdown and PDF** should contain functioning links to these actual repositories.

**Collaborator-centric, like a couture atelier.** Cambrian Radiation's proposed ethos is not a standardized software factory: its organizational structure bends to the availability, curiosity and capacities of independent collaborators, rather than forcing people to mold themselves to a corporate hierarchy. This applies equally to the fictional workshop around Antonio; researchers and gifted independents may contribute episodically on terms appropriate to them.

**Technology as a generator of charitable autonomy.** An ambition for the real-world technologies and their fictional horizon is to make frontier capabilities available to independent NGOs, **including permission to earn commercial income from the technology**, provided that most profits support the organizations' charitable purposes. The aspiration is for thousands of financially capable, independent humanitarian organizations, not thousands of controlled subsidiaries. The core belief is not that beneficiaries must be good *to the inventor*, only that they may be good **to someone**. **'A inteligência benevolente, esteja onde estiver, tem que ter HPC.'**

**A concrete illustrative scenario, not a documented deployment:** a secondary-school mathematics teacher in **Eswatini** begins with slow, untuned Python to correlate school dropout rates with drought. Marabunta, insofar as workloads can actually be partitioned safely, aims to **parallelize work rather than demand that the teacher first optimize everything**. Later the teacher learns NumPy; an older European scientist volunteers valuable Fortran routines; the project may grow into localized climatic-risk research for mountainous communities. Scientific validation, ground truth and responsible communication are indispensable; a useful outcome is an aspiration, not an achieved claim. The teacher's questions and expertise in his community are as valuable to the example as computational resources.

**Human dignity, not IQ worship.** The film refuses to equate intellectual capacity with human worth. It also refuses to replace worship of IQ with worship of creativity or wealth. Brilliant people can misunderstand evidence, act cruelly, repent, make jokes, and fail. Ordinary people can love, imagine, invent and contribute. Benevolence toward strangers—including people never likely to reciprocate—is fundamental to the artistic worldview.

## **DOING** — What makes this a creative-production and engineering problem

This dossier is also a small illustration of the kind of creative thinking a software engineer can bring to work alongside directors, designers, animators, editors, sound teams, and AI-production specialists.

- **The audiovisual grammar is structural.** These are narrative music videos, not conventional scenes with songs pasted behind them. Motion, scene cuts, expressions, and musical phrases must be designed together. Selected passages must be able to abandon music entirely.
- **Visual fidelity is deliberately uneven.** Stick figures and rough animation can be the right art direction, while the intimate human tableau demands a dramatically higher standard. Technical workflows should preserve that intent rather than flatten every scene to an identical look.
- **Character and narrative continuity survive style changes.** A viewer should recognize a character's choices and temperament even when they are rendered as a crude drawing in one shot and as a highly sophisticated figure in another.
- **Localization is part of the image.** Dialogue, comics, covers, signs, WhatsApp messages, and even typography integrated into animation frames should be localized for each language; subtitle-only adaptation is insufficient. This is an uncompromising design ambition, subject only to serious budget or schedule constraints.
- **Human creative authority remains central.** AI-assisted pipelines may help with asset generation, tracking, versioning, editing variants, and selective re-rendering, but they do not get to replace the judgment of the creative professionals defining the work.
- **Adversarial review is productive.** A compelling image is not enough: if it is unearned by the plot, or if a beat trivializes a serious character event, it should be revised or cut. The creative process should welcome critical challenges and rejected drafts.
