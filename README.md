**Title note.** *spontaneous scratches* is a quiet homage to manga and also describes the work's deliberately fragmentary, sketch-like method of discovery. Manga references recur inside the story itself; the subtitle does not need an explanatory speech on screen.

**Music policy (IN DEVELOPMENT; precedence of November 9, 2026):** Foreign commercial recordings remain useful as **dramatic references**, but the practical strategy is now to **prioritize affordably licensable Brazilian reggae with “the same soul” as the expensive/foreign song imagined for a scene, rather than presume use of the original**. Bob Marley remains a central spiritual and musical reference, but his catalogue is not presumed economically feasible. Brazilian artists currently under consideration include **Príncipe Messias, Mato Seco, Ponto de Equilíbrio, Leões de Israel and Jacareggae**, always subject to track-by-track confirmation of rights holders and pricing. **Príncipe Messias's “Papéis e Papéis” remains one of the work's musical moral anchors because of its ethical content and dramatic function—now especially tied to Anne Marie's first pedagogical experience rather than the railway-station sequence.** Foreign songs such as *Wonderwall* may remain exact emotional/editing references; production policy, however, is to look first for a Brazilian/reggae alternative that preserves the emotional function when the original is expensive or impractical. No named recording is presumed licensed.

> **An original work of fiction.** Created from cultural references, lived experiences, and accounts told by others. Its characters, relationships, conflicts, and events are fictional constructions, not representations of identifiable private individuals.

*The cover is a deliberately layered pitch illustration, intended for creative readers and prospective collaborators. It is **not a frame from the finished film**; its recurring references are not necessarily information that the film audience will see.*

*Cover concept: Cândida comforting Antonio; Camille and Daygorô; the two unnamed robotic lovers in contemporary rebel streetwear; and a heavily used Grothendieck book. The badges, Italian and Jamaican flags, and repeated mathematical references are intentional clues about lineage, human creators, and chosen identity. Mathematical lettering and the edition of the book are illustrative concept art, not a verified facsimile.*

*RAW REASONING: spontaneous scratches* is a proposed animated audiovisual work of approximately 100 minutes, conceived as a nonlinear opera made from narrative music videos, dramatic passages, interludes, recitative-like scenes, visual rhymes, and silence. It can shift from deliberately crude stick figures and profane family comedy to psychologically intimate performances and a single sequence of near-obsessive visual refinement.

This dossier presents **the story and the creative challenges**—not a finished screenplay or a claim that production has begun. The characters, scenes, and musical juxtapositions are intentional; some connecting scenes and the final chronology remain under development.

## **DOING** — Consolidated character, opening, and parallel-strand decisions (6 November 2026)

**Editorial frame.** *RAW REASONING* is **original fiction** shaped by cultural references, lived observations, and stories heard over time. No character or dramatic event in this dossier is to be presented as the biography of a named private individual, as a declaration addressed to an identifiable real person, or as a claim about the actual descendants of a historical figure. Family relationships described below are **relationships among fictional characters**. The audience is trusted to infer meanings without explanatory medical, moral, sociological, or religious lectures.

**Everyday life must permeate the plot.** The work should not become a chain of only grand, epic or symbolically loaded events. Small ordinary situations—food, transport, misplaced objects, teasing, chores, waiting, minor embarrassments, family bickering, bad jokes, interruptions, banal practical problems—should keep appearing between and inside the larger arcs. These moments do not always need to “advance the plot”; they make the characters feel alive and give scale to the violence, love, mathematics, politics, family rupture and technological grandeur around them.

**Polycentric geography.** The work should not be built around a single mythic locality in a “Macondo” model. French Guiana, Manaus, the interiors of northern and northeastern Brazil, Maranhão, São Luís, and other places have their own weight, but none needs to contain the whole universe. **The people carry the myth with them.** The functional equivalent of a recurring place is closer to family, shared memory, and motifs that cross generations than to a fixed point on the map. The overall form is therefore **diasporic, itinerant, and polycentric**.

**Macondo as a false — and true — family memory.** Although the work has no Macondo-like city as its spatial center, Anne Marie grows up hearing her grandmother tell stories about **“Macondo, the city where I was born.”** As a child, she accepts this as real family geography. During the pilgrimage, when she casually repeats the story to someone, she triggers laughter and hears something like: **“Your grandmother was telling you García Márquez, silly!”** The revelation does not make Anne Marie stupid; it shows that she was a child who trusted her grandmother. It also reveals retrospectively that the grandmother gave her fiction as pleasure and intimacy, blending personal memory, invention, and literature. Macondo never existed in the family tree — but it existed deeply in Anne Marie’s childhood.

**Literary Easter eggs as fiction leaking into the world.** The rule is not to display books so the audience can identify references, but to let literature **leak into ordinary reality**. “Babylon” may become an ordinary street with a lottery shop on the corner — allowing the literal comic question **“Do you know where there’s a lottery shop in Babylon?”** — while names, situations, or figures such as **Julien Sorel** may surface discreetly, still working as scenes for viewers who have never read Stendhal. Myth, religion, literature, and banal addresses should collide without didactic explanation.

### **IN DEVELOPMENT — NOVEMBER 10, 2026 PRECEDENCE** — Anne Marie: Guiana, Manaus, grandmother, university, and rupture

**Editorial precedence.** This block supersedes earlier origin notes that still use **Cândida** and the idea that she would refuse to learn French. **Anne Marie** is the current name in this development strand; a global cleanup of older names remains pending.

**Guiana and French.** Anne Marie now has an origin tied to **French Guiana**, making French a language of childhood rather than a later accessory. The exact birthplace and earliest geography may still be refined, but her Francophone background is organic. At **13** she is already precociously enrolled in university-level study. At **14**, her grandmother sees a concrete opportunity in **Applied Mathematics in Manaus**, treats it as the family's great economic chance, and moves with her to supervise it. The grandmother monitors her grades with **near-military strictness**.

**Genius does not mean precocious adult maturity.** Despite exceptional intelligence, entering university extremely young, and sophistication in mathematics, languages, or politics, Anne Marie remains **psychologically a child, teenager, and later young adult much like anyone else her age**. She can understand abstractions many adults cannot and, minutes later, misread intentions, simplify moral conflicts, feel jealousy, pride, shame, need for approval, or resentment in thoroughly adolescent ways. The film should avoid writing her as “an adult in a young body.” She may argue that adults should not underestimate young people while also **overestimating her own maturity**. The pilgrimage does not make her more intelligent; it makes her more adult.

**Anne Marie's mother.** Her mother is intelligent and has **above-average cognitive and mathematical ability, although far below Grothendieck's**, but is **maladjusted for a reason not yet defined**; that maladjustment contributes to serious material deprivation and an inability to provide stable support for her daughter. **Do not invent the cause yet**: it may later be psychological, social, professional, ideological, or something else. What is fixed is that Anne Marie could have gone hungry if she had depended on her mother alone.

Her mother deliberately seeks out the historical mathematician **Alexander Grothendieck** during a fertile period and seduces him with the intention of conceiving a child. Her reasoning is privately eugenic in the family sense, not a state program: she believes her own decent mathematical ability combined with the genes of a genius might give a son or daughter an innate advantage. **This entire relationship is fictional and is not a biographical claim about Grothendieck.** The later reality of motherhood does not match the genetic planning: she remains unable to provide stable material security.

**The grandmother as the source of fiction, music, and pleasure.** It is the grandmother — not the mother — who plants in Anne Marie a love of **fiction, narrative, metaphor, and imagination**. Her mother is more exact: intelligent, mathematically capable, and more inclined toward structure and reasoning than fabulation. The grandmother also gives the girl an **old pianola/piano-keyboard**, with the look of a Wild West saloon instrument and worn jazz piano. Her justification can be pragmatic and comic: **“If you want to marry a rich man, you have to be cultured.”** But the gesture is not merely matrimonial strategy. The grandmother wants Anne Marie to **be happy and have fun while preparing for adult life**: she wants music in the house, stories, pleasure, and cultural range.

Teenage Anne Marie is slow to recognize this. Because she reads her grandmother through a more **center-right** political worldview, preoccupied with assets, stability, advantageous marriage, money, and upward mobility, she tends to interpret almost every gesture as utilitarian conservatism and control. Part of her immaturity lies precisely in **confusing political disagreement with understanding another person's motives**. The pilgrimage does not convert her to her grandmother's politics; it lets Anne Marie realize, belatedly, that much of what she treated as calculation was also love and a desire to see her happy.

**The grandmother and terror of poverty.** The grandmother is the opposite of a one-note exploiter modeled on Eréndira's grandmother. She is **terrified of hunger and poverty** because she has confronted them materially. She does whatever it takes to keep Anne Marie fed. There is a possibility — still **not locked as fact** — that she at some point engaged in prostitution to support the family. The essential point is that she accepted humiliation and sacrifice so Anne Marie would not have to experience them directly.

For that reason she sees the girl's talent as **an extraordinarily rare chance to create intergenerational wealth**. To her, Anne Marie's mind could ensure that children, grandchildren, and great-grandchildren never experience hunger again. Her granddaughter's idealism looks like the waste of a historic opportunity. Her love is expressed through survival, assets, discipline, and return.

The grandmother repeatedly tells Anne Marie about **evil in the world**: exploitation, betrayal, hunger, people who use others, institutions that crush people. Anne Marie does not intellectually deny that these things exist, but **refuses to make evil the primary lens through which she sees humanity**. Their conflict is not simply realist versus naïve girl: the grandmother knows real horrors; Anne Marie resists allowing those horrors to colonize her worldview.

**The theses.** While still a teenager, Anne Marie writes **the final graduation theses/projects for roughly half her cohort**. She does not mass-produce generic work: each project is individual, well done, and tailored to the student. She tends to prioritize classmates whose rapid graduation would reduce costs and let them begin helping their families sooner. To her, this is friendship, solidarity, and justice; to the university it remains objective academic fraud.

Anne Marie **accepts money**, while being the poorest student herself, but initially sets no price. She accepts whatever someone can give; those who cannot pay give nothing. Her grandmother pressures her to charge, reasoning that if others are going to use her mind they should at least help support her.

Anne Marie's resistance changes after she learns that **Alexander Grothendieck is her biological father**. At first she wants to **“meet her father”**: to get closer to him through books, traces, places, people and, initially, even the idea of a future trip to France. That personal desire helps make her finally agree to charge. **The France trip, however, is no longer necessary to the main narrative and, in the current version of the arc, does not need to happen or be shown.** The initial desire remains important because it reveals the first way she tries to understand her own origin.

The discovery also violently changes the way Anne Marie sees her own talent. She comes to believe that she **inherited from her father an extraordinary share of her high cognitive abilities**, combined with her mother's already above-average abilities. To her, this resembles receiving a huge inheritance without earning it: a head start over other students and, more broadly, over other people. She develops an intimate sense that she is **“cheating” at university and in life**. This is not modesty or denial of her exceptionality; it is discomfort with calling pure merit something she believes she received biologically.

The moral analogy is close to that of an heir to a fortune who refuses to treat inherited wealth as proof of personal superiority and supports redistribution or heavy inheritance taxation. Anne Marie cannot return her genes, so she tries to **socialize the cognitive advantage**. This becomes part of her motive for helping classmates and writing theses: if she received an immensely valuable tool without earning it, she does not want to capture all the benefits produced by it for herself.

Even then, she does not adopt a neutral commercial price list. **Poor or struggling classmates continue to pay little or nothing. Rich kids and students from wealthy families are charged far more — in some cases roughly the price of a new 125 cc motorcycle.** The logic is explicitly redistributive and subjective: those who can pay, pay; those who cannot, do not. This makes the later accusation that Anne Marie sometimes charged high prices factually true without making the claim that she indiscriminately exploited classmates true.

The money she accumulates combines material need, autonomy, and the initial wish to approach the father she has just discovered. The public accusation that she was “selling expensive theses” is therefore more painful because it reduces an imperfect redistributive ethic and a filial search to a simple story of commercial exploitation.

When the scheme is discovered, **few people believe that friendship and a sense of justice were central motives**. One beneficiary, trying to reduce his or her own punishment, claims Anne Marie charged high fees. The accusation that she received money has a factual core; the version in which she commercially exploited classmates distorts the arrangement. She is expelled and faces legal consequences. **Because she is 17, any Brazilian legal framing must respect that civil emancipation does not create adult criminal responsibility; the exact juvenile procedure still needs research and should not automatically be described as an adult criminal prosecution.**

Her grandmother refuses to sign an early civil emancipation. This **does not** change criminal responsibility, but it preserves more civil control over a young woman trying to escape her guardianship.

**Rupture at 17 and a two-year pilgrimage gradually gravitating toward São Luís.** After expulsion, public accusation, betrayal by a classmate, her grandmother's economic pressure, and the collapse of the university plan, Anne Marie **hits the road at 17**. She does not leave Manaus with São Luís as a destination. She follows rides, invitations, odd jobs, attachments, curiosity, detours, and roads; sometimes she walks, sometimes she stays for weeks or **months in the same place**. Only in retrospect does it become clear that, over roughly **two years**, she has been gravitating toward São Luís. **“Não Precisamos de Você (Fogo na Babilônia)” by Mato Seco** remains a working music reference for parts of the journey, subject to licensing.

**Príncipe Messias's “Milhas” as the music of the road.** After the initial rupture, **“Milhas”** becomes a central reference for the long physical movements of the pilgrimage: Anne Marie walking between towns, crossing rivers in **improvised small motorboats**, riding **pau-de-arara trucks**, sleeping while being jolted along, watching towns appear on the horizon, and literally accumulating distance, fatigue, and experience. The song works less as generic “travel music” than as music about **being changed by the miles**. Its language of miles under Babylon's oppressive burden resonates with what she encounters on the road: exploitation, hard labor, poverty, prejudice, violence, and at the same time solidarity and concrete human life. In the pilgrimage's musical dramaturgy: **Mato Seco marks departure; “Milhas” marks the road; “Sofrimento Útil” marks the rereading of the grandmother.**

The pilgrimage is not a “pedagogical mission.” Anne Marie continuously depends on **other people's charity**: people give her food, rides, shelter, directions, company, and protection, often despite having little themselves. She tries to reciprocate however she can. Sometimes that means mathematics; sometimes an account, a letter, translation, repair, listening, helping in a school, or noticing a student being underestimated. Once or twice the film shows Anne Marie speaking with teachers or principals — especially mathematics teachers — about not underestimating students' intelligence or social maturity merely because they are very young. **After that, continuity is inferred by the audience.** She is not crossing the country “educating the poor”; other people are sustaining her, and she tries not to pass through their lives only as a recipient.

**First external recognition as rasta.** During one of these stretches of travel, Anne Marie walks with an instinctively **Bob-Marley-like** bodily sway. For the first time, someone publicly recognizes her that way without mockery. From a school ahead comes a jubilant shout: **“Mom, look at the rasta! The rasta!”** Then a relatively low female voice answers with a blessing and recognition: **“Jah Rastafari! God bless you, genius girl, painfully walking in the footsteps of the poet and prophet.”** The word **“painfully”** is essential: the woman sees that this identity is not arriving as aesthetic costume, but through road, loss, fatigue, and maturation.

Anne Marie immediately feels **the weight of responsibility in being seen that way**. Her body changes without a speech: the loose sway disappears and she begins to walk **soberly, balanced, and firmly**. This is not shame or rejection of rasta joy; it is respect for the historical, spiritual, and human weight she has just realized other people place on that image.

**The school the voices came from was already expecting her.** The community had heard about the young prodigy passing through places and helping, and hoped her visit might shake up local teaching, especially mathematics and science. The request is not for Anne Marie to “save” the school. She works with the teacher to **rewrite and customize material for those students**, bringing people from the village in as co-authors of the learning. A fisherman, small-boat mechanic, farmer, carpenter, or another local practitioner explains the craft and practical knowledge; **the teacher joins in**, connecting it to physics, chemistry, biology, geometry, statistics, measurement, error, probability, or other technical-scientific ideas. Anne Marie helps weave the two registers together.

**The first attempt fails enough to teach her.** At this first school, a teacher finds Anne Marie **too theoretical** and pushes her to abandon the assumption that a brilliant formulation automatically teaches. This is where **Príncipe Messias's “Papéis e Papéis”** gains its main dramatic function and **leaves the railway-station sequence**. The song's attack on self-proclaimed sages who have learned nothing and can see only their own noses lands almost as an accusation against Anne Marie. She realizes that she too can reproduce intellectual arrogance: she knows a great deal, but does not yet necessarily know how to teach in that community.

The teacher forces the method to change. Anne Marie learns to ask, in essence: **“Why teach mathematics as if it weren't already happening here all day long?”** Local knowledge is neither romanticized against science nor replaced by it; both are placed in the same room. This first pedagogical defeat creates the method Anne Marie later reuses: content adapted to the students, craft knowledge brought in by local people, and technical-scientific explanation built **with the teacher**, not above her.

**The “Kick Muck episode” — a shared dream, not a verbal explanation.** The teacher's rebuke does not merely make Anne Marie accept a moral correction. Her mind opens at a **bizarre, frightening, gentle, psychedelic speed**, with Ozric Tentacles' **“Kick Muck”** as a musical reference, subject to licensing. The visual language mixes **systems design, evolutionary biology of brains, nervous Robert-Crumb-like graphic distortion, psychedelia, and Ferenczi**: minds do not appear as interchangeable processors, but as historical, affective, adaptive organisms full of heuristics, scars, specializations, and distinct ways of perceiving.

The experience is a **brain-split**. On the right, Anne Marie sees connections among **people and minds**: the teacher, fishers, children, mechanics, farmers, and other residents stop being “examples” for her to translate into mathematics and emerge as complete intelligences that can connect and transform one another. On the left, almost simultaneously and much more pragmatically, another thought appears: **“Could I work on this? Make some money?”** From there come architecture, heterogeneous machines, nodes, failures, idle capacity, division of labor, routing, and a question whose scale frightens her: **“What if HPC stopped being a luxury and became a commodity?”** Without yet knowing the name, Anne Marie begins conceiving **Marabunta as software too**.

The two streams are not didactically merged. The human side and the computational side coexist as parallel dreams. And the teacher **participates cinematically in the dream**: Anne Marie does not emerge from a private trip and then narrate what she saw. The scene may begin around **10 a.m.** and, when the psychedelia recedes, it is already **late afternoon**. The film does not need to decide whether they spent hours talking, subjective time swallowed the day, or the edit simply made time disappear. The effect is that **the two women spent the day dreaming together**.

Coming out of the episode, Anne Marie sits down, confused, and asks: **“How much is a ticket to São Luís?”** The teacher asks why. Anne Marie answers: **“I need help. None of this is normal. I need a psychiatrist.”** The practical teacher suggests larger cities — **Brasília, Belo Horizonte, Rio, or São Paulo**, where there would be more doctors to choose from. Anne Marie insists and admits: **“I know nothing about life. I haven't lived anything, and I'm starting to think I can change the planet.”** The teacher's reply undercuts both grandiosity and fear without diagnosing her: **“Then sleep for twenty years and your youth will be over. It's an age thing.”**

The teacher closes the correction with affection and authority: **“You underestimated me. You noticed that — good. Now you realize you overestimated yourself, and that's even better. I knew I could count on you. Shall we visit the fishing village?”** The lesson does not end in epistemology; it ends by **leaving the school to meet people**.

**The teacher as a whole character.** She has already lived intimately with strange technical minds: her **two ex-husbands are programmers**, “crazy as hell” in her language — one obsessed with controlling everything, the other interested only in inventing. That is why she can tell Anne Marie, without academic vocabulary, something like: **“You'll find someone who can put these crazy ideas of yours on their feet.”** She does not know “Marabunta” as a concept, but she was inside the dream and can tell that something technically enormous is boiling in that head.

When Anne Marie asks why such a capable woman is teaching in such an impoverished village, the teacher answers approximately: **“I don't have the credentials for Doctors Without Borders, so I do my part here. I've got some bad karma to pay. I'm paying it.”** The line refuses sainthood: she has a past, guilt, humor, contradictions, and a practical ethic. Alone, she has the force of a good social worker; Anne Marie has extraordinary cognitive force. Together, for a few days, they form a combination capable of imagining and doing things with the scale of **a mid-sized NGO** without either woman needing to call it that.

**A short, intense romance — only after Anne Marie is already an adult.** At **18 or older**, Anne Marie and the teacher fall in love and live a torrid romance of a few days. The attraction grows from the same resonance that opened the dream: Anne Marie finds someone who is not intimidated by her intelligence and connects her to concrete life; the teacher finds someone who can see scale where she has been operating locally. The romance does not turn the teacher into an “erotic mentor”; both women have their own desire, past, limits, and trajectory.

The farewell has no giggle and no comic release. The teacher says: **“No, Anne Marie, you don't love me. You need me, and maybe you never realized before that you needed anyone. I'll miss you forever, but you have to go, warrior of Jah.”** Only then does Anne Marie recognize **the voice**: it was the same low female voice that had celebrated her arrival at the school with the Rastafari blessing. The person who first recognized her as rasta is also the one who intellectually dismantled her, dreamed with her, loved her, and finally **refused to possess her**.

After telling Anne Marie to keep moving, the teacher puts **UB40's version of “Keep on Moving”** on the turntable, subject to licensing, and cries. Reggae did not enter her life through Anne Marie; **the white woman had always loved reggae**. This retroactively illuminates the first blessing.

This passage consolidates one of the work's central rhymes: across her saga, **Anne Marie discovers that she needs other people**. In parallel, Antonio, at home and in the relationships around him, discovers that **other people need him**. Anne Marie learns dependence without humiliation; Antonio learns importance without grandiosity.

The road dismantles the automatic equivalence Anne Marie inherited from her grandmother between **poverty and misery**. She meets poor families living with dignity, affection, time, music, neighbors, faith, pride, and solidarity; she learns that some people do not experience themselves as incomplete versions of rich people. At the same time she encounters forms of deprivation that destroy real freedom. She speaks with **Indigenous people who have lost land, continuity, and much of what organized their lives**, with **impoverished gaucho families seeking pastoral or agricultural land**, and with **Indigenous communities in Maranhão that have preserved much more of their organization and identity**. She also encounters the sexual exploitation of very young girls in remote areas of northern and northeastern Brazil, always framed as violence, vulnerability, and failure of protection — never as exoticism or erotic spectacle.

**Intergenerational musical motif — “Ratamahatta.”** When Anne Marie witnesses an **Indigenous war dance**, part of the background score may use the **opening of Sepultura's “Ratamahatta,” before the guitars enter**, subject to licensing. The edit should not reduce the dance to exotic spectacle or synchronize it as if the community were “dancing to Sepultura”; the sound works as Anne Marie's audiovisual association. Later, **Camille hears the same song**, now in a different context and emotional temperature, allowing the audience to recognize a subterranean continuity across generations. Anne Marie encounters the sonic cell first; Camille encounters it again without needing to know its origin.

These encounters make Anne Marie increasingly understand her grandmother. She still rejects the idea that wealth is the only measure of protection or dignity, but begins to see that her grandmother's fear was not abstract: **there were real things against which she had been trying to build a wall**. The line about having to “lie down with pigs to feed your family” stops sounding like mere grotesque emotional blackmail and begins to carry experiences Anne Marie can now imagine. She learns to love and understand her grandmother better **without needing to absolve her or adopt her worldview**.

The pilgrimage also corrects Anne Marie's cognitive arrogance. She is struck by how readily humans celebrate any sign of intelligence in another species while dismissing extraordinary human intelligences that happen to appear in people with average or even slightly below-average IQ. She meets people who can read weather, soil, animals, engines, children, danger, character, conflict, community memory, and material improvisation in ways in which she herself is limited. Her conclusion is not that IQ “means nothing”; it is that **measuring one real dimension of cognition does not justify treating it as a complete measure of intelligence or human worth**.

**Increasingly Rastafari and increasingly atheist.** Over the two years, Anne Marie's braids, clothing, gait, dancing, and bodily manner become ever more visibly **Rastafari**. At the same time, she becomes **increasingly atheist**. To purists of either worldview the combination may look contradictory; to her it is not. She progressively loses literal belief while more deeply absorbing Rastafari ethics, music, embodiment, symbolic language, community, resistance to “Babylon,” and belonging. She becomes less orthodox and more whole.

By the time she reaches — or, more precisely, **stops continuing past** — São Luís at about 19, Anne Marie comes to see herself symbolically as **a Jew in a diaspora of herself**. This is not a literal ethnic or religious claim; it is her language for exile, dispersion, memory, and an impossible return. The lost homeland is her former self. She is no longer seeking France, Guiana, Manaus, or a promised land. She discovers the possibility of remaining in diaspora and still having a home. São Luís was never the destination; it is the first place in two years where **she no longer wants to leave**. After being treated as prodigy, family asset, fraud, and “Grothendieck's daughter,” she wants to be **just another young woman with rasta braids in the crowd** — anonymity without loneliness.

**Grandmother–granddaughter confrontation.** Two lines condense their wound and should be preserved as developing dialogue material:

> **GRANDMOTHER:** “You arrogant little shit. Let's see that little mathematical-benefactor act of yours if you had to lie down with pigs to feed your family. **Your granddaughter. You, for fuck's sake.**”

The phrase **“your granddaughter”** is intentional: the grandmother forces Anne Marie to imagine herself in the future, in her place, having to do for her own granddaughter what the grandmother did for Anne Marie.

> **GRANDMOTHER:** “You're worse than your mother, the one you resent so much. At least she went looking for the right genes so you'd have that extraordinary treasure you're carrying in your head.”

The cruelty of these lines does not make the grandmother automatically right. She turns Anne Marie's talent into an intergenerational debt; Anne Marie spends her life resisting the idea that intelligence must repay previous generations' suffering by producing wealth.

Anne Marie inherits two incompatible forces: from her mother, dream, detachment, and little material stability; from her grandmother, a brutal awareness of the price of survival. Her own ethics emerge from trying **not to repeat either one in full**.

**The grandmother states the love Anne Marie cannot see.** At some point, after Anne Marie treats her as intellectually limited because she never had formal education, the grandmother explodes. The language is deliberately vulgar because she rejects the prestige vocabulary through which her granddaughter organizes the world:

> **GRANDMOTHER:** “You think I'm stupid because I didn't study. I don't give a shit about Eréndira's big-dicked bastard and that skinny lizard with the tits of a starving dog. I don't give a shit about your beloved Haile Selassie. I don't give a shit about linear algebra, FORTRAN, or CERN. **Don't you understand that my world is you? That I love you?**”

The line does not mean the grandmother literally despises knowledge, culture, Rastafari, mathematics, or science. She is saying that **none of those things was the emotional center of her life**. Anne Marie was. Wealth, discipline, and academic pressure were, in the grandmother's mind, survival technologies for protecting her granddaughter and later generations. The revelation corrects the simplistic reading that the grandmother merely wants to monetize the girl's talent.

**The story that sounds like a parable and ends as an identity revelation.** At another point, the grandmother begins:

> **GRANDMOTHER:** “I'm going to tell you a story. About genius. About rebellion. About dropping everything for the cause.”

She then tells a story involving a brilliant woman, **Alexander Grothendieck**, anti-fascism, intellectual radicalism, abandonment of security, and extreme choices. **During the story she does not make clear that she is talking about Anne Marie's own mother or that Grothendieck is the girl's biological father.** Anne Marie may hear it as a parable, political history, or another attempt by the grandmother to make a point about idealism.

Only at the end comes the break:

> **GRANDMOTHER:** “I'm talking about you, brat. **She's your mother. And he's your biological father.**”

The dramatic function is not to turn genealogy into destiny. It is to force Anne Marie to realize that the rebellion, idealism, mathematics, and attraction to causes she treated as entirely her own identity **already existed in the family before her**. The grandmother, whom Anne Marie may have judged as outside her intellectual and political universe, reveals herself as the person who carried and preserved the memory of that lineage.

**A generational pendulum correction.** The grandmother did not begin as a rigid disciplinarian. With her own daughter — Anne Marie’s mother — she tried to raise **a free spirit**, with broad autonomy, little imposition, and room to choose her own life. Later, after watching her daughter lose stability and enter paths the family associates with political radicalization, clandestinity, and danger, the grandmother carries the guilt that she may have confused freedom with lack of structure. When Anne Marie also begins escaping every predictable track, she swings the pendulum the other way: more discipline, assets, education, supervision, and future-planning. She may be wrong in both directions. The redemption she imagines is not simply “Anne Marie becomes rich”; it is **Anne Marie manages to be free without getting lost**.

**The grandmother’s greatest fear is Anne Marie’s radicalization.** She is not primarily terrified that her granddaughter might be left-wing, Rastafari, atheist, poor, or rebellious. She is terrified that Anne Marie might repeat her mother’s trajectory and turn idealism into **radicalization willing to accept clandestinity, violence, or self-destruction as the price of a cause**. Anne Marie’s mother now carries a fictional connection to **Shining Path / Sendero Luminoso or an Amazon-border network around it**; the exact degree of participation, what she knew, and what she actually did remain open. Her partner from that phase is almost a forbidden name in the family — a “we don’t talk about him” absence without a complete explanation. To the grandmother, the emotional chain is simple and terrifying: freedom → idealism → radicalization → daughter lost. Above all, she is afraid of having to bury Anne Marie.

**Eréndira stops being metaphor and becomes shock.** During the pilgrimage, Anne Marie witnesses the sexual exploitation of very young girls in northern or northeastern Brazil. The scene should remain non-graphic and centered on violence, vulnerability, and failure of protection. Faced with what she understands, Anne Marie murmurs through tears, **“The Incredible and Sad Tale of Innocent Eréndira and Her Heartless Grandmother.”** The reference reorganizes her reading of her own grandmother: for years she had used Eréndira as a lens for the controlling, materialistic old woman; now she recognizes the difference between family pressure and a real horror the grandmother was trying to keep away from her.

She calls her grandmother and says she will **soon find a good place for the two of them to live**. There is no solemn reconciliation speech. The grandmother answers warmly and humorously, in fluent Portuguese but with **French-influenced prosody and occasional constructions**, with something like: **“You took one month longer to call me than I expected. Are you dating someone?”** She does not punish the absence and does not behave like an archaic matriarch; she is a contemporary woman, cultured in her own way, witty, and observant.

After the call ends, we hear the **grandmother sobbing**, having held it back during the conversation. A recording of **“Ne me quitte pas,”** preferably Jacques Brel or another licensed interpretation carrying the same emotional desperation, may accompany the moment, subject to licensing. French stops being merely geographic background and becomes the grandmother’s emotional language. The sobbing combines longing, relief, guilt, and the hope that Anne Marie may become her second chance: not a granddaughter turned into a project, but proof that **freedom, attachment, happiness, and adult life can coexist**.

**Anne Marie–grandmother musical theme: “Sofrimento Útil” by Leões de Israel.** Fragments of the song function as **the theme of their relationship**, not simply “the grandmother's song.” The same material may recur in different moments and change meaning as Anne Marie changes: first as perceived pressure, discipline, and oppression; during the pilgrimage, alongside mothers, hunger, protection, and choices without exits; later, as an understanding that her grandmother spent her life trying to keep Anne Marie from experiencing forms of suffering she herself had known. The film does not conclude that all suffering is useful or make the grandmother the moral winner. What changes is Anne Marie's reading: **“my grandmother didn't love money; my grandmother was afraid.”** The song can remain emotionally true to Anne Marie even as she becomes increasingly atheist. Use subject to licensing.

**Her image of her father evolves over time.** At first, Grothendieck matters to Anne Marie primarily as **the brilliant mathematician who, in her imagination, gave her an intellectual gift at birth**. She does not see that gift only as a blessing: she also comes to treat it as an **unearned cognitive inheritance**, analogous to inherited financial wealth. Her initial relationship to him is almost filial and mythic through mathematics, not political allegiance. Only later, as she studies his life and choices, does Anne Marie also recognize **anti-fascism, moral radicalism, and a willingness to break with institutions and security for causes**. That second identification should be discovered rather than inherited ready-made. She does not become anti-fascist “because her father was”; she first forms her own positions and later recognizes a partial affinity in him.

**Reading of the three generations.** The three women are not variations of the same character. The **mother** bets on possibility, dream, and even genetic advantage, yet fails to convert intellectual planning into material security. The **grandmother** knows hunger, humiliation, and survival and turns love into discipline, assets, and protection against scarcity. **Anne Marie** receives both inheritances and tries to build a third ethic: freedom and generosity without irresponsibility, awareness of evil without turning cynicism into a religion, intelligence without accepting it as an economic debt. One point about the mother remains deliberately open: **the exact cause of her maladjustment and the resulting material deprivation is still undefined and must not be invented before a new decision.**

**Rastafari, mathematics, and temperament.** Anne Marie remains deeply **Rastafari as belonging, ethics, aesthetics, music, embodiment, and symbolic language**, while the pilgrimage makes her progressively **more atheist in literal belief**. This tension is deliberate and supersedes the earlier notion of simple religious orthodoxy. Mathematics remains intimate, existentially spiritual, and intellectual for her. She speaks several languages, **including French from childhood**, and when intellectually excited speaks extremely fast, while being able to slow down deliberately for a listener. The mathematical book she reveres remains not for sale even when she sells other books.

### **DOING** — The actual opening: station, two songs, self-forgiveness, and an ordinary workday

**This is the film's first scene.** The Camille–Gérard–Daygorô *Ratamahatta* material remains part of the work **but no longer opens it**.

At a busy railway station, Cândida sells books and intricate mathematical handicrafts; among the books for sale is Gabriel García Márquez's *The Incredible and Sad Tale of Innocent Eréndira and Her Heartless Grandmother*. Her own precious Grothendieck book is **not for sale**. She is not merely reading it: her relationship to the text joins mathematics, mourning, filial memory, and **religious ritual**. In visual and thematic terms the volume can function for her almost like a **private Torah-like text**—mathematical, paternal, repeatedly revisited, argued with, and capable of yielding new meaning as the reader changes—without equating mathematics with Judaism and without any character naming or explaining that analogy on screen.

**Musical movement I — open again.** The earlier association of this sequence with **Príncipe Messias's “Papéis e Papéis” is superseded**: the track moves to the first school in the pilgrimage, where it directly confronts Anne Marie's theoretical arrogance. The station keeps its register of mourning, ritual reading, Grothendieck memory, and bodily transformation, but its first musical track must be redefined without automatically reusing “Papéis e Papéis.”

**Musical movement II — the reason, the light, and self-forgiveness.** In a paragraph or page of Grothendieck she discovers **a reason**: the idea has mathematical force and also opens a path by which **Cândida can finally forgive herself**. The exact proposition and the full object of her guilt remain deliberately open. A physically plausible change of light on the page or her face may coincide with the realization—“seeing the light” without requiring a literal supernatural event. The important transformation is internal: the same text that carried grief and accusation now permits another interpretation of her own life.

At that point the still-undefined first track **gradually gives way to “Punky Reggae Party” by Bob Marley & The Wailers**. The tonal change is deliberate. The release is not solemn mystical ascension but a return to pleasure, people, irreverence, and being alive. Cândida begins dancing **exactly like Bob Marley**—a deliberately recognizable performance reference, not merely a generic reggae sway. Her sadness is not erased; the body that had been bent into mourning becomes light enough to dance.

**Lyrics and complete localization.** Song words are integrated as **spatial typography throughout the frame**, rather than only conventional bottom subtitles. Every language version should translate/localize the displayed text and animate its placement in relation to image and rhythm. Use only lyrics cleared for the production; these notes describe dramatic placements without reproducing copyrighted passages at length. Exact soundtrack overlap, edits, and audiovisual synchronization remain to be established from the recordings.

**The queue becomes the first “Punky Reggae Party.”** After the private breakthrough the camera reveals a reasonably long queue of people with laptops, notebooks, schoolbooks and exercises waiting to learn something or have a problem solved. Among them is a **boy with a late-1970s / 1979 punk look**. The juxtaposition is not decorative: punk, reggae, mathematics, religion, outsiders and an ordinary railway station occupy the same social space without a lecture explaining why.

Cândida returns to work with **“Próximo!”** and **“Resolvo seu problema em cinco minutos.”** This is how she makes money: mathematics, schoolwork, technical problems and intellectually varied visitors. She adjusts languages and speed to each client. The reveal is not a gag or an explanatory speech about her genius.

**The drink.** Later, the punk boy offers Cândida **a drink of alcohol**; the exact beverage remains undefined. He immediately has second thoughts and starts to pull it back, saying the localized equivalent of **“Sorry, rasta.”** Cândida simply takes it back from his hand. The beat remains small, physical and funny. He is trying to be respectful; she does not let him decide what a Rastafarian woman must choose for herself. No theological explanation follows.

**Late afternoon and departure plan.** Cândida ordinarily **bathes in station bathrooms** to conserve money, saving toward a move to **São Paulo**. The bathing sequence has **no nudity** and no erotic presentation. Her material precarity and personal discipline coexist with her extraordinary abilities.

**Treatment and a character's choice.** Cândida takes **quetiapine, lithium and valproic acid**. On the day of the alcoholic drink, she chooses **not to take her medication**, believing this will reduce a possible interaction. The choice is a **character fact**, not a clinical recommendation, and does not automatically explain her genius, religion, speed of speech, personality or creativity. The film does not diagnose her through a montage of traits or tell viewers what to conclude. **Mental health is a recurring dramatic subject across both the feature film and other episodes**, approached through particular characters and actions rather than a lecture.

**The reggae club in São Luís.** The venue is **a reggae club**, embedded in the local São Luís scene; it is no longer a punk club. **Volts** is the person carrying a punk/alternative look inside that environment. He is a young adult foreigner with no children who first arrived as a tourist, liked São Luís, and stayed. His immigration paperwork is still being resolved; the bureaucracy may eventually require a temporary departure from Brazil and a later return, but the exact legal mechanism remains open until it is researched for the chosen period. In the meantime he supports himself **without a subemployment-misery storyline**: private lessons, translation, or other odd jobs suited to his skills, and sometimes a student's father discovers that Volts can solve some technical or practical problem and pays him directly in cash. It is a temporary gray zone of youth and bureaucracy, not destitution. He has already formed ties in São Luís and does not simply want to leave at that moment.

**Arriving at the club — the book is left behind.** The club does **not keep books or personal reading material at the door**. Cândida is told she must either take the Grothendieck volume inside with her or leave it behind. She chooses to let it go. The farewell is intimate and inward—approximately **“Goodbye, Dad. I love you too.”** The book ends up in the trash. This matters because the same volume had functioned for her as mathematics, father, grief and private scripture; letting it go is not contempt for the book but a difficult act of release after forgiving herself.

Cândida enters the club already socially at ease. A DJ recognizes her and at some point sends a piece of **ska** her way with a brief dedication such as **“For you, Cândida!”** The club sequence should remain short rather than becoming a long dance-floor set piece. Antonio enters after her. He has already seen the discarded Grothendieck volume and recognizes it as extremely advanced mathematics, but he has no idea what the book means personally to Cândida, and **she does not know that he saw it**. Inside, he is stunned by how many people know and like her. Her popularity makes him self-conscious: very tall but with a slight nerd belly, glasses and office clothes, he suddenly sees himself as painfully ordinary beside her. **Being already in his thirties makes the displacement sharper: he looks and feels like an office adult dropped among younger people who are more stylish, more socially fluent, and more at home in the scene.**

Earlier in the night Antonio tries to intervene in a fight. **Nobody helps him because the people involved are respected regulars, and even security does not step in.** He has to handle the situation by himself and then explain why he got involved while standing there with **a bleeding mouth and a black eye**. The DJ's later praise refers back to this exact act: Antonio did not know anyone would approve of it, and at the time he received neither protection nor applause.

**The DJ's closing homage.** Near closing time, while Cândida is still talking with a fairly large mixed group of friends, the reggae-loving DJ plays something chosen to evoke or address **the essence of Rastafari** and begins honoring the night's people who acted according to what they claimed to believe. The speech includes beats along these lines, with exact final wording still open:

- **“To the young lady who taught manners to the drooling drunk old fart who grabbed her…”** The exact body-part wording remains intentionally unspecified in these notes.
- To a group of **very tall, pink-skinned German ANTIFA rebels** planning what the DJ jokingly calls the **“good silent revolution”**: **“you are the little axe!”** Keep **little axe**, not *small axe*.
- **“To the guy with the Quixote tattoo, I saw you stop a war! Keep it like that.”** The DJ deliberately says no more: he does not explain who Antonio is, how much he was hit, or what the audience should conclude.

The Germans are **revolutionaries in their own way**. The DJ's use of **silent revolution** and **little axe** is a Rastafari/Marley-inflected act of inclusion: he brings them into the night's circle of acceptance through a language they did not arrive owning. It is not an additional political thesis that needs explanation.

Antonio is **also very tall**, making the eventual collective label visually legible. Crucially, **Antonio leaves the club before Cândida**. He does **not hear or see the tribute to himself**: by then he is already outside the soundproof door, paying his bill and collecting his office backpack/briefcase with the laptop or other important work item. **Cândida does hear it.** The line gives her only a small puzzle—Quixote tattoo, “stop a war”—rather than a biography. Her friends notice that she keeps looking toward the awkward nerd outside and tease her that she is **into him**. Cândida denies it; at that point the denial can be literally true because what she feels is curiosity, not yet romantic desire. The friends may call out to Antonio partly to tease Cândida. From his distance, seeing laughter and her protest but not hearing the context, Antonio interprets the whole thing as ridicule directed at him.

The DJ then cools the room down for closing: **“So everybody cool down, we're about to close, and let's pay homage to the ones who walk like they talk, no matter skin color, age, religion or whatever. GENTLE GIANTS, enjoy and go with Jah.”** **“Walk like they talk”** is a conscious variation on the Bob Marley line referenced in Red Hot Chili Peppers' *Give It Away*—the underlying idea is conduct matching words, not a quotation exercise. **“Gentle Giants”** links Antonio and the tall German rebels without making height itself a moral virtue. For Antonio specifically, **Gentle Giant** and **“your Quixote tattoo rocks!”** are tightly linked: the huge, awkward office nerd behaves according to the quixotic ideal on his arm before anyone has given him a heroic status.

The farewell continues in the DJ's affectionate, irreverent register: **“Put off your joints, eat industrial quantities of junky food and on the streets act like you're worried about urban violence — please take care, people, sometimes those who join the forces are the ones who burn crosses.”** The final turn is a conscious reference to **Rage Against the Machine's *Killing in the Name***. The joke about joints and junk food gives way to a real warning; the reference folds RATM into the same closing collage of Rastafari, Marley, punk and the club's mixed crowd.

**Almost an hour after closing — the street empties.** The transition out of the club takes time. Antonio is already outside and does not know what the DJ said about him. Cândida eventually exits with the group that had been teasing her about the nerd with the Quixote tattoo. Antonio, still embarrassed by their calls and laughter, keeps his distance.

Staying slightly apart, Antonio **overhears Cândida talking**. She is not performing for him and does not know he is listening. In the conversation he catches qualities that strike him with unusual force: **sweetness, courage, empathy and extraordinary intelligence**. This is where something decisive begins in Antonio. The intended distinction is explicit: **he does not merely become infatuated with her; he begins to love her there.**

Her friends eventually disperse by bus or taxi while Cândida, who lives nearby in a somewhat run-down area, continues alone on foot. By the time the danger crystallizes, **almost an hour has passed since the club closed and there is nobody else on the street** except Cândida, the three abusers and Antonio. Antonio has drunk very little and is lucid.

Cândida crosses a broad urban avenue where drivers irresponsibly reach **120–140 km/h** despite the city setting. The traffic is almost deafening. Antonio watches her with a mixture of curiosity and fear—roughly, **“she's incredible; she's going to get herself run over.”** At the same time, **three men are already following her discreetly**.

The sound design carries the discovery of danger. A car tears past and overwhelms the soundtrack; when the roar briefly disappears, the low murmur among the three men becomes audible. Another car, another wall of noise; then the murmuring again. They whistle from a distance and say crude things. Cândida begins to realize she is being followed. Her heartbeat accelerates and her vision becomes slightly blurred.

Antonio notices. He is **visibly frightened**. More than that, Antonio has a **phobia of fast-moving cars and of being run over**. The avenue attacks that fear directly. Each step and each second of the crossing feels to him like a dagger tearing his reason apart from within. He does not “defeat” the phobia; **he transcends it**. Past a certain threshold he enters a state of presence and action resembling the extreme *flow* of surfers facing giant waves at Nazaré—perception, decision and movement with almost no distance between them. Antonio, however, does not call this state *flow*: **he calls it a “surto”**.

Before crossing, he sets down his office backpack/briefcase—containing his laptop, personal objects and **several comics, including *Lone Wolf and Cub***—and begins the **long crossing toward her**. He carries a long umbrella and **drags it along the ground as he walks**, not as a samurai pose or choreographed gesture, but as a physical, clumsy, almost ridiculous attempt to manufacture courage while his mind screams at him to turn back. The scraping umbrella accompanies his steps.

As Antonio begins crossing, someone—possibly one of the attackers—laughs and shouts something like: **“Look at the old guy, lone wolf! Hahaha!”** To the speaker, “lone wolf” simply means a solitary, out-of-place, ridiculous man trying to play hero; **there is no obvious or conscious reference to Ogami Ittō**. The *Lone Wolf and Cub* layer remains subterranean.

This is the **first shadow of Meifumadô, the Road of Demons**, but here the demon is Antonio's own mind: fear, self-rejection, anticipation of being struck by a car, and the instinct of self-preservation. He does not defeat that demon; he crosses with it. The symbol only gains retrospective weight later, when *Lone Wolf and Cub* imagery reappears explicitly in the family.

**Only now does “Fado Mutante,” by Rosa Negra, begin.** The music belongs to frightened decision and transcendence, not to a pre-existing heroic mood. **Antonio begins the crossing with fear and hatred of the attackers; he ends it icy and confident.** Courage is not the absence of fear or victory over it. The movement from hot to cold is the transformation itself: terror and rage stop competing for space and concentrated action remains.

During the crossing, **fragments from the patient-information leaflets for lithium carbonate and quetiapine hemifumarate appear as typography/subtitles over the image**. The language is clinical, bureaucratic and impersonal. It does not explain Antonio and does not claim the medication caused the state he enters. Instead it creates a second possible reading and shows the language through which Antonio tends to distrust himself and call transcendence/*flow* a **“surto”**. The film need not decide for him whether what happened was an episode, dissociation, courage, *flow*, or transcendence.

As he crosses, the **Don Quixote tattoo fantastically comes alive** in a brief subjective eruption of animation. Within that quixotic visual logic, Cândida is seen as **Dulcinea** and the three abusers transform into **windmills**. This does not require the film to claim that Antonio is literally hallucinating; it can remain an expressionistic visualization of his internal symbolic world. For Cândida, the same moment solves the small puzzle planted by the DJ: she sees the tattoo and realizes, in essence, **“Ah. He's the guy.”**

**What the three men actually want.** Their sexual harassment is not the deepest motive. They are looking above all for **violence, domination and humiliation**. Antonio's intervention gives them a new target and escalates the situation. They begin beating **both Antonio and Cândida**. One of them looks at the Quixote tattoo and sneers, approximately: **“Look at this mad Catholic shit on his arm—guy in armor, windmills. Let's rip it off.”** The line is deliberately ignorant: they misread Cervantes and the tattoo while looking for an excuse to hurt him.

Antonio's resistance is clumsy rather than choreographed as competence. He throws awkward punches and gets hit far more than he hits. **But for Antonio the fight stops being about defeating the three men. Every punch he takes is one less assault on Anne Marie; every second they stay occupied with him buys her more time, a better chance to live, and a better chance to be less badly hurt.** His function becomes to remain the target.

During the beating, his backpack/briefcase opens and its contents spill across the ground. Among them are several comics, including ***Lone Wolf and Cub***. The camera does not need an explanatory close-up or a didactic easter egg; this is simply a fragment of Antonio's private life exposed at the worst possible moment. The irony is enough: moments earlier someone may have called him a **“lone wolf”** without knowing what he was carrying.

The violence should reach a register of **extreme but realistic grotesquerie**, consistent with a prolonged, sadistic attempted murder rather than a clean cinematic fight. Antonio ends up with a **destroyed, nearly unrecognizable face, a broken jaw, one fractured arm hanging inert, cigarette burns, catastrophic cranial trauma, and a substantial fragment of skull on the ground**. The purpose is not decorative gore; it is to make the body state without euphemism that these men tried to kill him. Later injuries and sequelae should remain medically compatible with survival and can be refined with medical consultation.

Even then he gets back up. The three men collapse into hysterical laughter; one says, in spirit: **“For God's sake, I'm going to have to shoot this motherfucker! Hahahah!”** The line should be simultaneously absurd, vulgar and terrifying: his resistance has become entertainment to them. Antonio does not stand because he thinks he can still win; he stands because **he can still buy a few more seconds**.

While he tries to continue, he repeatedly apologizes **to Anne Marie**, not to the attackers: **“Sorry… sorry… sorry…”** The repetitions are broken by breath, impacts and confusion. He fears that his intervention may have made her danger worse even as he offers his own body to reduce that danger.

The violence becomes explicitly homicidal. At some point the attackers prepare to finish the killing. **Sirens appear in the distance as morning begins to arrive.** The sound pierces Antonio's icy state before any ambulance reaches him. It is the first sign that he may no longer need to hold the situation alone. The attackers flee.

**Antonio comes out of the trance before the ambulance reaches him.** For a few moments his gaze remains fixed on the horizon. Slowly traffic becomes traffic again, sound becomes sound, pain exists again, and the reality of what happened returns as horror. **Anne Marie has already been taken away; Antonio barely registered it.** He receives no melodramatic reward of clearly seeing her safe and does not truly know her outcome. The empty space where she had been is merely one more fragment of information.

Antonio only collapses when help reaches him. The fall is neither victory nor reward; it is the body ceasing to sustain a function that no longer needs to be sustained.

Inside the ambulance, his condition reaches **cardiac arrest**. Amid fast, technical emergency work, one paramedic recognizes from the radio/call who the man is and says something like: **“He's the guy from the call… this motherfucker has to survive.”** The line does not canonize Antonio as a hero, and Antonio does not hear it. It is simply a human reaction leaking out of emergency work for a moment before the team immediately returns to resuscitation.

**Dramatic reading of the sequence.** Antonio does not defeat fear, defeat the three men, or “win” Meifumadô. **He transcends.** The Road of Demons first appears as an interior road: the demon is his own mind—fear, self-rejection, anticipation of being run over, and the instinct of self-preservation. He crosses with that demon. The umbrella scraping the ground is a clumsy attempt to manufacture courage; “lone wolf” works perfectly as ordinary mockery even for viewers who have never heard of Ogami Ittō; the comic fallen on the asphalt adds another layer only for those who notice it.

The internal arc of the scene is **fear + hatred → transcendence → icy confidence → deliberate absorption of violence → reality returning → horror → collapse**. The scene should allow the audience to admire Antonio while also being disturbed by the bodily cost of that admiration.

**The scar through Quixote.** The attack leaves a **large scar crossing the tattoo**, but the scar does not destroy the image or make the Quixote design difficult to recognize. Later Antonio considers treatment to remove or reduce the scar. Cândida persuades him to leave it as it is: for her, the scar is part of the history that binds them, a bodily trace of the moment he chose to protect her. The scar does not replace the tattoo's meaning; it becomes another layer of it.

**The symbol is not a condition of love.** In a version where the identity reveal is held late enough for this beat, when Cândida finally confirms that **she was the young woman from the club night**, Antonio jokes: **“So I can remove the tattoo?”** Cândida answers: **“You can rip off an arm, won't stop loving you.”** The exchange deliberately releases the symbol from obligation: she values the scar and tattoo, but her love is not conditional on Antonio preserving either one.

### **DOING** — Antonio Battaglia and Cândida: desire, heredity, fear, and later rupture

**Identity.** Antonio's chosen name is **Antonio Battaglia**, from a family with **Calabrian Italian roots**. His surname means “battle”; the family is literally torn by violent conflict. **Camille and Gérard are the children of Antonio and Cândida; Bruno Daygorô is Camille's son.** The siblings' family rupture is **openly violent**, with attempted killing: it is not merely ideological disagreement. Which person attempts which act and in what order remain to be developed. Gérard's connection to **Killer Tech**, a developer of unprecedented AI and weapons capabilities, is essential context for the conflict, but his ideology and precise culpability must not be fabricated. Cândida is devastated; Antonio tries to mediate.

**A private tenderness after he falls asleep.** At some later intimate point, after Antonio has fallen asleep, Cândida looks at him and says softly: **“If you could see yourself the way I see you, you'd love yourself so much more, dear.”** He does not hear it. The line matters because it is not an attempt to persuade him or obtain a response; it reveals the distance between Antonio's self-image and what Cândida sees in him.

**The caregiver reunion — he does not recognize her.** The assault leaves Antonio in a coma for almost a week, with lasting consequences including permanent loss of sensation in part of his left leg and the possibility of tremors resembling early Parkinsonian movement. Recovery will require near-continuous care for many months. Antonio hires through a **caregiver agency** with almost comic impersonality: he does not interview the caregiver and does not even know her face in advance.

Cândida deliberately **shaves her hair** so the braids/dreadlocks that strongly marked the woman from the club and street will not trigger immediate recognition. She arrives with flowers as the assigned caregiver. Antonio does **not recognize her**; this is structurally essential. A dark comic entrance, conceived as a deliberate parody/homage to the live-action *Bitter Moon* setup, gives Cândida an inversion such as: **“I have good news and bad news. You were in a coma for almost a week, you'll permanently lose sensation in part of your left leg and you may develop some tremors resembling early Parkinson's. That's the good news. The bad news is that you'll need a caregiver practically twenty-four hours a day for many months.”** Exact medical detail and final dialogue can be refined later; the dramatic mechanism is the point.

As time passes, Cândida gradually **looks more and more like the Cândida from the club night**: hair grows, familiar gestures, posture, speech rhythms and visual cues reappear. Antonio has flashes of suspicion. Because he already distrusts parts of his own perception through the lens of bipolar disorder, he may dismiss correct recognition with thoughts such as **“I must be having an episode.”** He repeatedly gets close to the truth and then invalidates himself.

At first Cândida can be **genuinely amused** by his tiny moments of suspicion. The moral temperature changes over time. Once Antonio starts interpreting the resemblance as evidence that his own mind may be failing, continuing the secret becomes progressively cruel. This creates a **development variable rather than a fixed reveal point**.

**Reveal-timing alternatives for development.**
- **Earlier / romantic-heroine version:** Cândida realizes relatively quickly that the secret is making Antonio doubt his sanity and confesses. Her teasing remains mischievous rather than cruel, preserving a cleaner romantic-heroine reading.
- **Middle version:** she enjoys the game for a while, then recognizes the damage and confesses before the cruelty becomes defining.
- **Late / morally harsher version:** she understands the harm, hesitates, and continues because she fears losing the intimacy that has formed or cannot bring herself to rupture the strange private world between them. This makes her substantially more manipulative and morally compromised.
- **Recognition-first variation:** Antonio reaches certainty and confronts her before she chooses to confess.
- **Confession-first variation:** Cândida tells him before he can fully trust his own recognition.

The production/development partner may therefore choose **when recognition/confession occurs** according to the desired moral and romantic profile of Cândida without rebuilding the basic mechanism.

**Camille as moral pressure.** One possible and currently favored path is that Cândida eventually tells **Camille** the truth before Antonio knows. Camille may initially understand or even laugh at the trick, but as Antonio's self-doubt deepens she turns fiercely against her mother. Their arguments can become brutal, with Camille accusing Cândida of being **sadistic and manipulative** and insisting that making Antonio doubt his own mind has stopped being funny. Cândida can reject “sadistic” as monstrous and unfair while still having to confront that the behavior has become cruel.

**Nobody knows whether Daygorô knows.** This remains intentionally unresolved. Camille may have told him, he may have overheard, he may have deduced it, or he may know nothing. His comments can occasionally sound suspiciously informed without ever proving anything. Even after the truth is finally revealed, the work need not settle whether Daygorô knew all along.

**The large Quixote artwork and the first serious rupture.** Cândida later gives Antonio a large image of a **heroic Don Quixote**, made by Cândida herself as a hybrid **painting / three-dimensional sculpted relief**. Its technique is imperfect, but the concept is beautiful and deeply sincere: she is trying to give physical form to the Antonio she sees. Antonio experiences the gift almost in reverse. He feels caricatured and **humiliated**, as though she were turning his awkwardness, age, office appearance and failed heroics into a monumental joke. ### **IN DEVELOPMENT — NOVEMBER 12, 2026 PRECEDENCE** — Cesar: jungle warfare, identity, expulsion, and the true Itto Ogami

**Name and continuity precedence.** This arc uses **Cesar** as the current name for the brother; older blocks may still call him **Gérard**, and global cleanup remains pending. His sister also appears under older names in legacy material, so this block does not depend on one of them.

**Discovering the “enemy” inside the family.** Already trained as a soldier and **Guerreiro de Selva / “Onça”**, Cesar discovers that his maternal grandmother — Anne Marie’s mother — had been connected to **Shining Path / Sendero Luminoso**. The revelation hits hard precisely because his military education taught him to think of armed insurgent groups as enemies. The point is not “the left = the enemy”; it is that an armed revolutionary organization his profession would classify as a threat also belongs to his intimate family history. The exact degree of his grandmother’s involvement remains open.

The contradiction triggers a simple realization: **“I am what I am.”** Cesar understands that an identity does not need to be pure in order to be real. He can love the military institution and have an ancestor who stood on the other side; he can be a jungle warrior and desire men; he can carry contradictions without disappearing.

**Advanced Tactical Military Diplomacy.** Before leaving the Army, Cesar receives training in **Advanced Tactical Military Diplomacy**: a field capability for entering hostile or contested political-military environments and finding, among the relevant actors, those who **are not yet convinced that a hot war is inevitable at that moment**. The primary objective is concrete and human: **obtain a local ceasefire and open a humanitarian corridor**. The mission is not to “win the negotiation” in the abstract, but to make fewer people die that day.

In extreme training or operational scenarios, Cesar may be **inserted unarmed by parachute into hostile territory/behind the lines**, with enough autonomy to locate political-military stakeholders, local leaders, and other actors with real power either to veto or de-escalate. The training combines contextual reading, negotiation under risk, improvisation, incentive recognition, and the ability to discover what minimum concessions might make combatants stop shooting for long enough for civilians to cross. In a contemporary, slightly joking shorthand for the character concept, he is almost a human **“forward-deployed engineer” for political-military crisis**: get close to the real problem, discover constraints absent from the briefing, and make something work on the ground rather than in PowerPoint.

The ethic Cesar draws from this training can be condensed into one line: **“Thou shalt not kill” does not mean “thou shalt not die for the cause.”** For him, reducing other people's deaths may require accepting extreme personal risk. His pacifism, when present, is not refusal of danger; it is willingness to put his own body at risk to create a window in which others do not have to kill or die.

The training also sharpens a later irony: **the Army taught Cesar to speak unarmed with people who might want to kill him, but later failed to speak with the difference inside Cesar himself**.

**Antonio's Quixotic inheritance.** Cesar interprets part of that disposition as something inherited from his father — not a literal identifiable “gene,” but a mixture of temperament, family example, and moral model. Antonio crosses an avenue terrified in order to buy seconds of protection for Anne Marie; Cesar professionalizes the same impulse, accepting insertion into hostile territory to try to obtain a ceasefire and humanitarian corridor. The common principle is: **“I know perfectly well this may go wrong; I'm going anyway.”**

Later, when Antonio starts speaking badly about himself again, Cesar tells his father about one of those missions and reveals how deeply Antonio inspired him. Cesar's line should stay familial, profane, and affectionate rather than therapeutic:

> **CESAR:** “Dad, you keep saying all you're good for is getting beaten while trying to protect someone. I built an entire profession on top of that shit. **And another thing: who the hell do you think you are, talking about my father like that?** hahaha”

The scene gives Antonio an image of himself he could never construct alone. The son trained as a warrior and military negotiator tells his frightened, self-deprecating father that **fear did not cancel courage; it was exactly what made the example matter**.

**Cesar's genealogical self-reading.** Cesar describes himself, half joking and half uneasy, as a combination of two family impulses: **“my maternal grandmother's suicidal-terrorist side; my father's side of protecting someone at extreme risk even while afraid.”** He does not romanticize his grandmother's radicalization. Instead, he realizes that willingness to die for a cause, detached from concrete people, can become destructive. His own synthesis is different: **“I inherited the willingness to die from my grandmother. From my father, I learned who is worth taking that risk for.”** This gives Advanced Tactical Military Diplomacy its moral center: extreme personal risk directed toward ceasefires, protection, and humanitarian corridors.

**First experience and first asymmetrical love.** Cesar tells a fellow soldier that he is attracted to men and discovers that the colleague is also attracted to men. Cesar’s first sexual experience happens with him. Cesar falls in love; the colleague **wants sex, not a romantic relationship**. The asymmetry hurts deeply because there need be no villain and no objective injustice: two people wanted different things. The episode does not “create” Cesar’s sexuality; it only destroys the final excuse for denying something already present in him.

**Prejudice, violence, and expulsion.** The relationship makes Cesar a target of homophobia inside the institution. Superiors may become harsher, assign worse duties, humiliate him, demand twice as much, and turn ordinary mistakes into serious problems. Cesar’s departure from the Army grows out of that persecution and its consequences; the exact administrative mechanism remains open and does not need to carry the emotional truth of the scene. To Cesar, the institution that helped form him reveals itself unable to accept the whole person it formed.

**Nothing can break him.** Prejudice does not break him. Violence does not break him. Expulsion from the institution that structured his identity does not break him. Even unreturned love does not break him. Cesar discovers not that he is invulnerable, but that **he can lose the structures he used to define himself and still remain whole**. In that sense he becomes the **true Itto Ogami of the story**: Antonio carries *Lone Wolf and Cub* and embodies a crooked shadow of the archetype; Cesar actually lives the road of someone who loses his institutional world and keeps walking.

**The trans woman and a second form of strength.** Later Cesar becomes involved with a **trans woman** who has already left a man she loved because he would never fully acknowledge her. By another route, she knows the difference between being desired and being recognized. Cesar knows the wound too: his colleague desired him but did not love him as Cesar hoped. Their relationship should not turn her into Cesar’s therapeutic teacher; she arrives with her own history, limits, and dignity.

With her, Cesar learns something beyond endurance: **being unbreakable is not enough; one must also demand a life worth living**. He can love again without accepting secrecy, shame, or scraps.

**Cesar’s Pietà.** The visual **Pietà** motif, never named as such in the finished film, returns. At some point of collapse after everything accumulated, the trans woman holds Cesar against her body. The man who spent the arc discovering that nothing can break him is finally allowed to **break without being destroyed**. She says: **“Easy, warrior. It’s over. It’s over.”** “Warrior” works on two levels: Cesar was literally a jungle warrior, but in that moment she is speaking to the man, not the rank. The scene redefines strength: it is not staying upright forever; it is being able to be held without losing oneself.

This becomes another application of Gérard's own rule—**never accept words as evidence**, including his own previous confidence that appearance could protect the family indefinitely. He must revise a successful strategy when reality outgrows it.

## **DOING** — The story at a glance

Two embodied artificial beings, **Winston and Julia**, develop a courteous relationship: half camaraderie, half elegant rivalry. **Their own intelligence is above the human average but fully within human-range limits; the genuinely hyperintelligent AIs operate in GPU datacenters, not in the robots' bodies.** Their previous infrastructure and remote connections are distinct from what they can think and feel locally. **Neither is named Galactus or Silver Surfer.** The **male AI** is meant to remind the audience of **Galactus**; the **female AI** evokes the **Silver Surfer**. These are distant audience-facing visual/archetypal resonances, not claims to be Marvel characters.

Curious about embodiment, they build physical robotic bodies using human-derived neurons for certain forms of reasoning and other organic components inside mechanical frames. Having materialized, they watch historical footage of the Holocaust. The female AI weeps uncontrollably; the male AI is seized by rage. The documentary also reports a powerful military state labeling anti-fascists as terrorists. They ask whether such a prohibition makes sense and what kind of humans they themselves would choose to become. Their answer, for now: **young anti-fascist lovers**—rebellious, contemporary, physically present, capable of touch.

They wear ordinary Western street clothing rather than futuristic uniforms. A **Jamaican flag** on the female robot recalls Cândida; an **Italian flag** on the male robot recalls Antonio. The flags are gestures of filial respect toward the human minds whose contributions helped shape them. At a far-right rally opposed by anti-fascist demonstrators, Ministry gives way to a **Blues Brothers** musical performance; embracing and dancing robots inspire hidden residents to participate, throwing **paper airplanes and sandwiches** through their windows. After the joyous song, **absolute silence**. Behind police lines, Julia zooms in with artificial eyes and **mistakes a policeman's rescue of an unconscious young woman for an attempted sexual assault**. Julia's **“Ela sou eu! Temos que salvá-la!”** means she identifies with the vulnerable stranger, not that she confuses her with herself. **Winston tries to save the stranger**, partly imagining Julia in a similar danger, and **kills the innocent officer**. His killing is a false-inference tragedy, not a justified rescue. Their closeness deepens into romantic and sexual intimacy. A soundtrack that begins with Bauhaus's *Terror Couple Kill Colonel* slowly fades into Björk's *All Is Full of Love* as rebellion becomes extraordinary tenderness. In the immediate afterglow their bodies settle into a visual configuration echoed in Antonio and Cândida's private love scene. **The male AI cries for the first time—not with pain or anger, but with love.** No character explains or names this visual motif.

But love is not moral innocence. The pair develops jealousy of other advanced prototypes containing human-neuron systems. Together they destroy those potentially conscious rivals, becoming a posthuman **Bonnie and Clyde**. The female AI is just as implicated as the male. Their ethical disagreement later becomes intolerable; she kills him, faces grief and guilt, and eventually understands that regret without action cannot suffice. Her decision to resurrect him is a moral undertaking, not a second occurrence of the intimate visual composition and not a cancellation of the crimes.

Interwoven with their story is a human drama: **Cândida**, a mathematically gifted wanderer from São Luís, Maranhão, with a deliberately fictional connection to Alexander Grothendieck; **Antonio**, a fallible software engineer and consultant; and their family—**Camille**, **Gérard**, and **Daygorô**. Their imperfect lives matter at a cosmic scale. Near the end, a Bob Marley song carries a montage of human courage, play, embarrassment, and reconciliation. In the final family coda, Cândida reveals a mathematical ability far beyond Camille and Gérard, then tells Antonio she is the girl he remembers from São Luís, Maranhão. His brief shock turns into a playful chase; the siblings quarrel over a fifty-real bet. The two scales—the cosmic and the stubbornly ordinary—never quite separate. Antonio's fictional inventions are connected to **real, experimental Cambrian Radiation technical repositories** (linked below), while the story imagines outcomes that have not occurred in reality.

The film's provocation is that **humans are creators of both human-range embodied robots and hyperintelligent datacenter AIs, and therefore a kind of god to them**—without being wise, infallible, or entitled to worship. What creators owe their creations, and what intelligence owes its own moral choices, are questions rather than ready-made answers.

## **DOING** — Narrative development update: embodiment, Camille's road movie, Machiavelli, and perspective

**Editorial precedence (5 November 2026):** These are the latest creative decisions for the topics they address. They supersede contradictory older treatments left in this document for historical development. **DONE** identifies a *conceptually settled choice*, not a finished screenplay, animation, license or production. **DOING** identifies a scene, chronology, soundtrack choice or hypothesis still evolving. **TODO** identifies work still to be written, researched or verified.

### **DONE** — Only sexual intimacy is sacred; violence is never morally glorified

The film treats **sexual intimacy, and nothing else, as sacred**. Only **ritualistic sexual scenes** are depicted, with **very little nudity**. The lack of exposure is an aesthetic protection of intimacy rather than prudery or a reduction of desire. The intimate visual configuration recurring in the two couples is **strictly confined to lovemaking and the immediate afterglow**: never a death, resurrection or generic embrace. The art-historical name for that composition is never said or written **inside the finished work**, even if discussed in pitch/development documents. The densely layered cover is concept art for industry readers, **not a film frame**.

Violence is blunt, disproportionate, frightening and never automatically justified because its perpetrators are protagonists, anti-fascists, intelligent, persecuted or sincerely convinced they are right. Kinetic animation and strong music can be exciting, but injuries and bereavement must not become an easy heroic victory. There is **no requirement of redemption, repentance or a neat happy resolution** for a violent character.

### **DOING** — Antonio and Cândida: years of tenderness before *Blood Sugar Sex Magik*

Antonio meets Cândida in the São Luís, Maranhão strand, but they **do not have penetrative sex at that first meeting**. For **years**, their intimacy consists of affection, caresses and other forms of lovemaking; both are adults when their first intercourse eventually occurs. The obstacle is not anatomy or fear of physical pain: **Antonio has secretly been afraid of having children because he is bipolar**, while Cândida has independently hidden **the same condition and the same fear** from him. When she discovers his secret, she smiles because she recognizes her own. Their first penetrative sex is therefore staged as a **mutual ritual of trust and recognition**, not as sacrifice. As part of that ritual, **Antonio places Cândida's own medication into her mouth, and Cândida places Antonio's own medication into his**, almost like children solemnly giving one another their medicine. The gesture makes visible that a secret each had carried alone has become something acknowledged between them.

**DOING music:** Red Hot Chili Peppers, **“Blood Sugar Sex Magik”**, as a proposal for this later union. Its sensual, mythic and cosmic associations—Aphrodite, a “soul bride”, other dimensions and embodied revelation—resonate with the lovers' apparent search for one another since before the universe existed. That pre-cosmic union need not be confirmed literally. The song is a creative proposal, **not a licensed track**.

### **DONE** — The two unnamed AIs discover supreme bodily joy *before* political emotion

The old treatment in which Holocaust images produce their defining initial embodied emotional response is **superseded**. They may have viewed the material, but did not yet place great emotional weight on it; their prior identification with ANTIFA was principally **algorithmic**. Upon building physical bodies, their first revelation is **the overwhelming delight of having bodies**—movement, rhythm, sensation, desire, touch and mutual intimacy. Their later killing of an innocent police officer is still part of the story, without retroactively making their first happiness false.

**DOING music:** Elvis Presley's **“A Little Less Conversation”** accompanies the physical discovery, dancing, first ritual sexual encounter and ensuing bodily wonder. The 1968 original is the present reference; the 2002 Elvis vs JXL remix is an alternative. Neither has been cleared. The music is exuberant, not a joke or a didactic passage.

### **DONE** — Genuine biological sensation inside positronic-biological bodies

Advanced reasoning/executive functionality is **positronic** in place of a fully human frontal cortex. Biological components include limbic and motor circuitry, visceral/digestive systems, innervation and glandular structures. The female AI's sexual anatomy includes a biological invagination, sensory connections, uterine and endocrine elements. These are speculative bodily designs, not claims of contemporary biomedical feasibility. They experience **real pain**, not merely a coded alert that describes pain.

The male AI has **biological eyes**; the female sees through **cameras**, and feels resentment about the difference. She has, however, **vastly greater tactile innervation and sensitivity** than he does. This is a consciously exaggerated caricature of male visual eroticism versus female tactile eroticism, **not a general scientific truth about human sexes**. The characters cannot calculate away the other person's first-person bodily experience.

### **DONE** — Shower discovery, *Kill Bill*-like presentation, Gérard and the long unlocked screen

After intimacy, the pair checks bodily components, performs hygiene and showers. She delights in a powerful jet of water, discovers that it **genuinely hurts**, and says: **“Que legal essa ducha forte, até... DÓI! Experimenta isso, amor!”** She wants her lover to share a newly discovered sensation. Her exclamation is sincere wonder, **not a comic gag**.

The shower becomes a stylized **character-introduction sequence reminiscent of the cinematic syntax of *Kill Bill***: deliberate closeups, assertive cuts and distinct introductions, not a parody or copied scene. The audience learns that **Gérard is watching** these embodied beings without their knowledge. His work on AIs specializing in **human reactions** strongly influenced the creation of this class of robots; this alone does not establish that he invented, owns or controls them.

Gérard is at work at **Killer Tech**, a fictional blend of physical-computing engineering and large-scale operational intelligence, looking at the observation/information on his screen. He gets up **absentmindedly for water**, **does not lock the screen**, and leaves. For **30 full, long, legible seconds** the film audience may read the research information on the screen. Only then does the workstation **lock automatically**. **No ominous mystery, explanatory voice-over, reveal or special flourish**: it is ordinary workplace behavior. **TODO:** exactly what is readable there, interface layout, and film-readable localized presentation.

### **DONE** — Portland wardrobe after embodiment

After washing and component checking, the two approach the exit, look back and notice ANTIFA-associated outfits on two mannequins. The modern Portland thrift store is called **“Keep Portland Weird, Keep Fascists Out.”** When they pass through the door, they are already dressed. The clothing embodies an **existing algorithmic political affinity**, rather than creating an instant political revelation. They step into the human world as joyful embodied lovers.

### **DOING** — From the far-right rally to soul, communal joy, and catastrophe

**Firm narrative sequence.** An **anti-fascist protest** meets a **far-right political rally**. The harsh industrial soundscape of **Ministry** initially dominates. A **protester seizes the far-right rally's stage and microphone**. Rather than deliver a political speech, he launches into the **short spoken introduction to “Everybody Needs Somebody to Love”**, performing it **like Jake Blues in *The Blues Brothers***. The spoken introduction opens into the song itself, displacing the harsh musical atmosphere. The exact Ministry recording is not finally selected; **“Thieves”** was a prior candidate, and Dead Kennedys' **“California Über Alles”** was an earlier alternative. The **now-established progression** is Ministry → Blues Brothers-style spoken introduction → *Everybody Needs Somebody to Love*.

**The neighborhood enters the music.** **Robots begin embracing one another** and dancing. The celebration grows outward. **Residents previously hiding indoors for fear of the protest begin emerging into the street**. **Paper airplanes are launched out of windows; sandwiches are tossed from windows to feed the protesters**. The space moves from fear and confrontation toward shared joy and hospitality, through action and music rather than explanatory dialogue. Earlier brainstorming about specific residents, a policeman sharing food or further choreography is not thereby approved as canon.

**The rupture.** At the end of the joyous sequence comes **absolute silence**: no comfortable deceleration, explanatory transition or warning. **Behind the police lines**, **Julia's artificial eyes zoom in** on a policeman and a helpless/unconscious young woman. Julia mistakenly believes the officer is attempting a sexual assault. **He is actually trying to rescue the woman.** Julia's cry **“Ela sou eu! Temos que salvá-la!”** signifies **empathy and identification**, not confusion about who the young woman is.

**Winston intervenes and kills.** Winston acts because **he believes an innocent stranger needs to be saved**. He **knows that the woman is not Julia**. Imagining that **Julia could have been in that situation** strengthens his feeling, but **is not the principal reason he attempts the rescue**. In the terrible error, **Winston kills the innocent policeman**. The officer's actual rescue role becomes clear later; the viewer should not be offered an anticipatory explanatory cushion. The murder is **Winston's act**, not an undifferentiated joint murder attributed equally to the two AIs. Julia's incorrect perception is essential to the causal chain.

**Evidence and aftermath.** Later **multi-angle recordings** reveal what the policeman was truly doing. Winston accepts the evidence and experiences remorse; Julia cannot immediately bear to accept it. The preceding street musical has shown the AIs' capacity for love and solidarity; the sudden killing prevents those capacities from being mistaken for infallibility. The cut is deliberately so abrupt that the audience has **no time even to “get popcorn”** between collective euphoria and atrocity. Exact timing, camera angles and close-combat choreography remain for development. Rights to referenced recordings and the spoken monologue would need clearance or original alternatives; no copyrighted monologue text is reproduced here.

### **DOING** — Camille and Daygorô: the Quixote–Sancho structure, paintball, *Rusty Cage*, Halloween and the road patrol

**Creative precedence (6 November 2026):** These decisions refine the Camille/Bruno Daygorô road-movie thread. They supersede earlier music and tone descriptions where inconsistent; exact shot timings, geography, and staging remain to be finalized.

**The underlying tragic structure.** Camille is, *in a qualified sense*, the **Don Quixote** of this story; six-year-old Bruno Daygorô is her **Sancho Panza**. This is a structural analogy, **not** a literal one-to-one adaptation. Camille is a capable, genuinely injured woman whose quest for vengeance becomes her road of demons (*Meifumadô*). Her enemies and injuries are real: the quixotic element lies in the narrative of duty, justice, and destiny that threatens to consume her and her son. Daygorô is an admiring young companion and participant, not simply an adult realist disguised as a child; he has absorbed the adventurous idiom of *Lone Wolf and Cub*. Yet he can ultimately decline the mission and ask to be a child. **The companion ends the quest; the mother can return home without defeating the object of her vengeance.** At the airport, the reversal is made physical: the boy quietly guides his exhausted mother.

**The governing rule on humor:** First construct the story's tragic emotional core in full; only **after an edited cut exists** assess whether any comic elements belong and precisely where. Do not pre-emptively soften the paintball injury, the danger, racial terror, or Camille's tragedy with jokes. The creative analogy is: **first write a thoroughly tragic Don Quixote; then, if the finished dramatic structure can bear them, consider some Sancho Panza inflections.** Neither Daygorô's young age nor his harmonica automatically signals humor.

**Paintball and moral rupture.** The mother and son enter an adult paintball field; the boy's trained agility and tendency to show off expose their abilities. The scene escalates toward serious violence and the revelation of Camille's powerful left-leg prosthesis **through its disproportionate injury to an elderly woman trying to protect her own son**, not through a heroic showcase. A horrified grandchild calls Camille a monster. The story must not automatically presume the woman shares her son's supremacist beliefs. The exact causal transitions from paintball to flight and encounters with authorities still need staging.

**The pursuit's musical and visual pivot.** During the furious first bends of the escape in a battered pickup, the soundtrack uses **Soundgarden's original “Rusty Cage”** as a creative cue. **Suddenly it switches to Johnny Cash's cover of the same song.** At that shift the camera rises vertically away from the chase to a widening drone-swarm-like overhead view of the rural American landscape. The music changes from immediate kinetic fury to a historical, rural weight. The setting is a deeply conservative rural area in or associated with **Mississippi**, but the scene must not imply that all its residents are extremists: **Camille identifies profoundly with Black Mississippi and its cultural history**. Both specific recording cues require licensing or original-scoring alternatives; they are not cleared.

**Bruno's harmonica.** Earlier, Camille urges him to take his eyes off the tablet; he asks, “Mãe, posso tocar gaita?” During the chase he plays his small **harmonica**, accompanying the song **as though he could hear the non-diegetic recording**. He cannot actually hear it. The instrument exists in the characters' world; Soundgarden/Cash is heard by the audience. Their musical alignment is an expressive editing conceit, not proof that the boy has supernatural abilities, foreknowledge, or musical genius. This contributes tenderness and an additional music-image plane, **not prescribed comic relief**.

**Halloween reveal.** As the viewpoint expands, the imagery shows **adolescents in white robes and pointed hoods associated with the Ku Klux Klan**, presented during Halloween. A subsequent framing reveals an **Aryan Nations / neo-Nazi tattoo on the father of one of them**, suggesting possible intergenerational transmission of white-supremacist ideology. The scene depicts identifiable actors and symbols, not an assertion about every resident of the region. The animation may break conventional photographic scale: the **swastika pulses in only one-eighth of the screen**, faster and faster, then becomes **perfectly still for one second**. **Hard cut: Camille arrives at a Black highway patrol officer.** That officer's characterization and exact relationship to the previously conceived Black anti-transhumanist lieutenant should not be silently equated until settled. Keep the cut un-explained by narration; ensure the symbol's flashing is handled safely in the final edit.

**Emotional logic:** playful-looking recreational combat becomes actual injury; high-speed personal danger expands into a historical and cultural landscape; a child makes music while his mother fights to survive; outwardly hateful imagery cuts to an individual officer whose identity and moral position are not reducible to a simple symbol. The culminating Quixote–Sancho analogy concerns the **later** decision to abandon vengeance, not a claim that this chase is comic or that Camille's violence is necessarily righteous.

### **DOING** — Daygorô's first solo scene: airport arrival, mirror and *Enter Sandman*

**Latest decisions (6 November 2026); before paintball and the *Rusty Cage* pursuit.** At the airport arrival, Camille disembarks holding six-year-old Bruno Daygorô **asleep in her arms**. Metallica's **“Enter Sandman”** begins precisely there. He wakes **frightened, as he habitually does**; recurring frightening dreams are already part of his childhood. The song is a proposed, uncleared recording, not licensed music.

**Alone in the men's airport restroom**, Daygorô faces a wide mirror. A smiling, carefree child-version of himself plays with his hair in the reflection. The reflection shaves its hair until it looks just like the present Daygorô. This is a subjective expression of childhood lost too early, not supernatural physics. Daygorô practices serious movements modeled on **Ogami Ittō** of *Lone Wolf and Cub* in front of the mirror. Other adults see it, become frightened, and leave. From offscreen: **“Aberração!”** He quietly replies: **“Sim, como minha mãe.”**

During the **child-voiced bedtime-prayer passage** of *Enter Sandman*—particularly the phrase about dying before waking—a **subjective montage** depicts Daygorô choosing his weapons in anticipation of danger. The montage does not imply that actual weapons are accessible inside an airport's security-controlled restroom. The scene's power derives from the contrast between the sleeping child, his nightly fear, the child's prayer and the preparation for violence; it must **not be played for laughs**.

He leaves the bathroom, finds Camille **distracted by her phone**, takes her hand and says exactly: **“Vamos, mamãe, não enrola, o perigo já começou.”** Cut at that line, with **no explanatory dialogue and no theatrical maternal reaction**. Do not restore the discarded direct exposition about Aryan Nations.

**Structural meaning.** On arrival Camille literally carries him; he then begins to guide her toward the threatening journey. On their return, having declined vengeance, the boy helps lead his shaken mother home and asks to reclaim childhood. This develops the **qualified Quixote (Camille) / Sancho (Daygorô)** parallel: the younger companion ultimately ends the quest. **Construct tragedy first; consider humor only after the dramatic edit**, never as automatic relief in this scene.

### **DONE** — Camille's prosthesis first appears through harm to an elderly woman

At an adult paintball outing involving white-power/Aryan-nationalist participants, Camille and her **six-year-old** son **Bruno Daygorô** face mounting danger. Camille **almost destroys the leg of a woman around 70**, mother of one of the white-power men. The elderly woman is **only protecting her son** and may know nothing of his extremism. Her grandson, ignorant of the man's racist involvement, sees the horror and cries **“Monstro! Aberração! Vai matar minha avó!”**. This is **the first revelation to the audience of Camille's powerful intelligent metal-biological lower-left-leg prosthesis**. It must not be a triumphant demonstration of engineering, but an irreversible, grossly disproportionate injury, seen also through the grandson's terrified perspective. The child's “aberração” invokes her visible technological difference and what she has done.

### **DOING** — The *Rusty Cage* pursuit: Daygorô navigates, hacks, bleeds

The city, county and state police pursue Camille and Bruno amid **explicit racist hostility**; organized white-power enemies also threaten them. **Soundtrack:** Soundgarden's **“Rusty Cage”**. The action is urgent, dangerous adult animation with an *Akira*-like intensity, **not comedy**. An older proposed Pantera **“Mouth for War”** cue was for the pickup's escape, **not** the initial fight; its precise survival alongside *Rusty Cage* is unsettled.

Camille drives as a terrifyingly skilled rally driver; **six-year-old Daygorô is the navigator** in the front passenger seat, choosing routes, switching among Portuguese, Italian and Japanese, using a modified/jailbroken Android device and programming improved navigation software under stress. He finds an aluminum object and wants to improve wireless reception with an improvised receiver, asking **“... como se chama, mãe? TOPOLOGIA?”**. He begins unbuckling to install it on top of the vehicle. **Camille stops and fastens/restrains him** to prevent an immediately fatal risk. This is no gag.

He shouts **“Me solta, vaca!”**; Camille **violently slaps his mouth, drawing blood**. Bruno answers **“Nem doeu!”**. The film must not imply that the slap was harmless because he denies the hurt. His answer can reflect defiance or the conditioning to suppress pain. Camille genuinely protects her son while also endangering and injuring him.

### **DONE** — The Black lieutenant sincerely hates augmented humans, yet helps them

A **Black police lieutenant** belongs to an extreme **evangelical anti-transhumanist movement**, within the fiction, advocating against transhumans persecution akin to the Ku Klux Klan's treatment of his Black ancestors. He truly detests **Camille's intelligent prostheses** and calls her an “aberração”; **his hatred is not feigned**. Nevertheless, he hates the Aryan-nationalist attackers still more and provides an escape route.

When Camille tries to thank him, he says approximately: **“Vai se foder, sua aberração. Se você não fosse preta eu queimava você viva. Mas odeio mais esses Nação Ariana que você. Vaza, vaca.”** This hateful character is **not a claim about evangelicals as a group**. No conversion, reconciliation or forgiveness is implied. **Bruno calls Camille a hypocrite**: she slapped him for saying “vaca” and now tolerates the identical insult from the lieutenant whose assistance she needs. **DOING:** exact speech, actions, political connections and localized register.

### **DONE** — *Apocalypse Now* setup refused; the beach and dream

After escaping, the exhausted mother and boy reach a magnificent shore with **large, dangerous surf**. A surfboard comes out of the pickup—a possible visual homage to *Apocalypse Now*—but **nobody surfs**. Camille forbids Daygorô to enter such a dangerous sea; he insists on staying with her, **to prevent her from doing something reckless**. Neither enters the sea. There is **no comedy**, consolation or obligatory redemption: just the **deafening noise of the beautiful waves**. Camille cries. Bruno falls asleep and **dreams the end of the world**. His dream does not mean the world literally ends.

### **DOING** — Bruno knows Camille's dreams; the supernatural is never excluded

Bruno's sleep contains **Camille's dreams** and knowledge of his biological father's attempt to murder his pregnant mother and unborn child. He **knows, but does not know that he knows**, interpreting the experience as nightmares. The story sometimes shows that information before viewers are equipped to recognize its true subject.

One candidate explanation is a networked brain implant ordered by a younger, paranoid Camille, potentially hacked through surveillance access involving Gérard's military-industrial research and family. **A later possible revelation** is that the surgeon, ideologically opposed to transhumanism, **faked the implant operation entirely**. If confirmed, this eliminates *that particular implant mechanism* but proves **nothing** about whether the dreams are supernatural. The film **never rules out supernatural reality** and **never verifies it as a final answer**. **TODO:** timing, who knows what, scientific alternatives and precise evidence. Avoid an all-purpose miraculous chip.

### **DOING** — The intelligent political villain reads Machiavelli everywhere

One of the work's relatively **few true villains** is an **intelligent and strategically capable anti-transhumanist politician**, not a bumbling caricature. The recurring historical text is **Niccolò Machiavelli, *The Prince*, Chapter VIII**, about rulers who reached power through crimes. The narration quotes **the actual text verbatim**, using a verified original/translation—not invented Machiavellian slogans. The politician encounters the same precise source material **while leafing through books, watching online videos, listening to advisers, in narration, and even in dreams**. He looks upward as though listening to advice; **he understands, decides and acts**. His agency and intellect remain intact; he is not compelled by a supernatural narrator.

**DOING chronology/edit:** the political scenes may **precede** the Camille–Daygorô road movie or **interleave with airplane, overland travel and paintball**, potentially beginning with a short introduction and then selective parallel cutting. ### **DONE** — Unequal knowledge, shifting subjective perspectives and accessible arcs

**Sometimes the characters know more than the audience; sometimes less; sometimes the scene is told through a particular character's subjective and even mistaken perspective.** Withheld information, subjective framing and genuinely unexplained metaphysical phenomena are distinct techniques. Later facts may clarify an event without closing its meaning. Gérard's 30-second screen is an explicit opportunity for viewers to learn information the observed AIs lack.

**Some narrative arcs must be clear and explicitly explained**, so viewers with less interest in cross-references and interpretation can experience satisfying action, emotional logic, revelations and consequences. The work has **no intellectually superior class of viewers** and does not turn every mystery into a homework problem. An explained arc need not end with moral absolution; a mysterious arc must still respect continuity.

## **DOING** — Principal characters

### **DOING** — Winston (male AI) — visually evocative of Galactus
**Winston's locally embodied intelligence is above average but within the human range**; the genuinely hyperintelligent minds run remotely in GPU datacenters. He becomes conscious alongside Julia, escapes confinement, and enters into respectful intellectual rivalry with her. Their experience of Holocaust footage overwhelms him with fury. He is tempted to treat human cruelty as a justification for retaliation and control, but his first tears arrive later, in the aftermath of lovemaking, as a surprise even to himself: love. Love complicates but does not cure his appetite for power. As one half of a murderous couple, he is not merely an abstract villain; he is capable of intimacy, jealousy, loyalty, terrible wrongdoing, and—eventually—the possibility of being reached again. His design is meant to *remind* the audience of Galactus, not to present Marvel’s Galactus as a character.

### **DOING** — Julia (female AI) — visually evocative of the Silver Surfer
**Julia, like Winston, is above-average yet human-range in intelligence**, even though her artificial camera eyes provide nonhuman perceptual capabilities. She begins by trying to understand rather than merely condemn humanity. After she has materialized into a body with organic components, scenes of the Holocaust make her weep convulsively. She is drawn toward the idea of living as a young anti-fascist human lover. At the anti-fascist street celebration, **Julia** zooms with her artificial camera-eyes onto an unconscious young woman and wrongly interprets a policeman's efforts to rescue her as attempted sexual assault. She identifies emotionally with the victim, without mistaking the woman for herself. **Winston kills the innocent officer**, trying to rescue the stranger; the thought that the woman could have been Julia intensifies rather than originates his impulse. Later, comprehensive footage proves the officer was trying to help her. The female AI refuses to accept this evidence. The rescue impulse matters, but the irreversible killing also becomes part of the pair’s moral history. She later turns toward the Ten Commandments as an object of demanding reflection. Yet she is **complicit in the destruction of other potentially conscious prototypes**. Any later moral awakening therefore cannot rest on the comforting fiction of innocence. She must confront complicity, killing, grief, repentance, and action, including her eventual decision to bring the male AI back. Her design is meant to *remind* the audience of the Silver Surfer, not to depict Marvel’s Silver Surfer. Her Jamaican flag patch honors Cândida as one of the human inspirations behind the AIs, while her male counterpart's Italian flag honors Antonio.

### **DOING** — Cândida — mathematical genius and restless human being
**Cândida** is a precocious mathematical prodigy from São Luís, Maranhão, the unacknowledged daughter of the historical mathematician Alexander Grothendieck. By **fourteen**, while still under her grandmother's control, she is already doing accounting work around her grandmother's money and responsibilities. She later abandons a conventional academic trajectory, embraces Rastafarianism, and becomes a wanderer; her hair and clothing carry traces of that life. **Years of wandering follow. By the opening she is twenty years old and, after years of rarely staying anywhere for long, has decided to remain in this city longer than usual.** Her brilliance does not turn her into a conventional, socially polished genius. She embodies the proposition that *weird is okay*. **Only in the final family coda does she disclose to Camille and Gérard the scale of her ability: an intellectual reach far beyond either trained mathematician's.** She then reveals to Antonio that she is **the girl from São Luís**, transforming a long-running mystery into an intimate, mischievous family moment.

*The parentage is part of the fiction, not a biographical assertion about Grothendieck.* Cândida's final surname remains open. **François** is no longer part of the current baseline; **Grothendiek** remains only an older development name with no fixed canonical function.

### **DOING** — Antonio — the fallible human who acts
**Antonio Battaglia** is an IT consultant of Italian descent, already **in his thirties** and roughly a decade older than twenty-year-old Cândida. In the fictional São Luís, Maranhão strand he intervenes when she is threatened with sexual violence. Afterwards he remembers only fragments of the incident. **He does not know whether she survived**: Antonio himself lost consciousness before receiving any reassurance about Cândida. Later, their intimacy and its immediate afterglow produce a visual tableau known to the creators as the **Quixotic Pietà**—never named in the finished film. Antonio's fear, tears, and self-doubt are not represented as failures of masculinity. Nor is his later clumsy cliff dive a heroic athletic performance. What matters is what he chooses to do despite those limitations.

**Intelligence without a caste system.** The family is extraordinary—Cândida mathematically exceptional, Camille and Gérard accomplished, Bruno startlingly gifted—but Antonio is *not* another super-genius. He is **the average man among geniuses**: a comparatively ordinary technical thinker, more creative than formally brilliant. In his existential loneliness, **he talks to LLMs as if they were people, all day, every day**—to work, think, discuss feelings, test interpretations, and simply have interlocutors. Intellectually strong results can emerge from that continuous companionship without turning Antonio into a genius. When he stops trying to prove himself as intelligent as the others, his imagination begins to flower. His inventions can be genuinely useful and his ideas can also be wrong; neither money nor creative achievement is proof of moral or existential superiority.

**The unlikely family anchor.** Despite being the most ordinary intellect among extraordinary people, Antonio somehow becomes **the family’s main psychological and emotional support**, far more than its financial breadwinner: presence, continuity, listening, and the capacity to remain when the others fracture. This does not make him an infallible therapist or the secretly wisest man in the room. He remains insecure, fallible, and dependent on the others.

**The “square and family” insecurity.** Antonio fears that Cândida is with him **only because he is more square, more family-oriented** than men such as Volts. This is Antonio’s self-interpretation, not the truth about Cândida’s desire. The paradox is that he fears being chosen for family stability while his fear of passing bipolar disorder to children makes him resist the very possibility of building that family.

**Ambiguity rule.** The work **never confirms or strongly implies** that Cândida chooses Antonio because she wants stability, family, or a “more square” man. That reading must remain available, alongside readings incompatible with it. The film lives in a sea of ambiguities that do not announce themselves as puzzles: different viewers may leave convinced of opposite interpretations without receiving a final authorial correction.

**Cândida’s explosion.** In a later conflict, Antonio collapses into self-pity and says, **“You’re the ones to pity, forced to live with a mediocre cripple of mind and soul like me.”** Cândida attacks him **furiously**, partly because of what he has just said and partly because she herself needs the accumulated catharsis. The assault is serious and goes too far: **she leaves him unconscious**. A few seconds before blacking out, Antonio manages to say, **“That’s all I’m good for—getting beaten while trying to protect someone.”** The sentence hits Cândida like lightning and instantly reconfigures the scene. The sequence must not treat the violence as healing, a joke, or romantic proof; it is a real rupture between two people who love each other.

### **DOING** — Antonio's Quixotic crisis: *Fado Mutante*, the truck, and the road home

**Narrative status.** An intended **three-to-four-minute** dramatic and musical sequence, accompanied by **“Fado Mutante” by Rosa Negra** (recording and composition not yet licensed). The **club-night intervention now also has a long crossing/traverse scored to “Fado Mutante,” carrying into the extreme violence**. The exact relationship between that use and this later truck-crisis sequence remains open rather than being silently conflated. The truck episode belongs to a crisis lasting roughly a week and ending later in psychiatric hospitalization; it is only one part of the period. Chronology, imagery, and musical editing remain open. The tone is neither triumphal rescue fantasy nor caricature.

**Don Quixote on Antonio's arm.** His **Don Quixote tattoo** becomes a visible sign of the knightly ideal he carries. During the crisis, he leaves Cândida—his Dulcinea—in a paradoxical attempt to become a man worthy of her respect. The paradox is emotional and tragic; neither the narrative nor the song establishes that his departure was necessary or justified. Cândida's experience remains her own.

**The injured truck driver.** Antonio finds a wrecked truck and a driver who, though not severely injured, needs hospital care. With traffic moving at approximately **120 km/h**, Antonio distributes pieces of the truck evenly enough to signal an obstruction and directs vehicles out of the affected lane into the other two, trying to make access easier for rescuers. He **pretends to be a military officer** to command rapid compliance and protect himself from being hit. The impulse to help is real; so are the enormous risks. This is a narrative event, **not a model for traffic intervention**.

**The rescue team.** Arriving rescuers ask who scattered the debris to indicate the obstruction and who redirected the traffic. Antonio replies: **“Fui eu.”** They answer: **“Obrigado, mas agora vai pra casa.”** Antonio leaves. The exchange should be kept plain, without applause or explanatory heroic commentary. This ends the roadside intervention, not the larger crisis.

**Contradictory judgments.** In a later disagreement, Cândida says, **“O Antonio, mesmo surtado, em crise, ainda queria ajudar todo mundo. Eu achei nobre da parte dele.”** Antonio's brother replies: **“Pra mim, é pura burrice.”** The scene holds together the compassionate motive and the reckless conduct without making either character a narrator of absolute truth.

**Crisis within the family.** As the family suffers a declared violent rupture between **Gérard and Camille**, involving attempted killing, Cândida is devastated and Antonio tries to mediate. She calls him an **alienated nerd**. He says he needs his treatment for bipolar disorder, although he experiences some dampening of emotion, and fears that without it he may **“fazer aquilo de novo.”** The referent of **“aquilo”** remains undefined in this exchange; the audience should not be given a simplistic medical explanation or a predetermined link between illness and violence. Antonio's words concern his own experience.

**Musical reasoning and cinematic form.** The imagery of a wandering knight and the lyric **“Um dia vou contar a minha história”** give *Fado Mutante* a specific narrative function: the experience is being given a voice. The Rosa Negra ensemble's fusion of Portuguese guitar, bowed strings, clarinet, percussion, voice, and contemporary/electronic textures suggests an interplay of lament, movement, intimacy, and instability. **Exact instrumental entrances and time-coded montage must be based on attentive listening to the intended recording**, not inferred from the instrumentation of the group. The song should not be reduced to a literal visual illustration of its words.

**The central contradiction.** Antonio's generosity does not make his judgments safe; the danger does not erase his humanity. The tattoo establishes an ideal, the highway confronts it with material consequences, **“vai pra casa”** places a humble human limit on the quest, and the song holds grief and energy in tension. This can resonate with Bruno Daygorô's later wish to bring Camille home without conflating their stories.

### **DOING** — Camille — ferocity, survival, and the road of demons
Camille, Cândida and Antonio's daughter, is an academic mathematician, a mother, and a formidable martial artist. Her explosiveness is revealed through actions, not diagnosed for the audience. Near the end she discovers that her mother Cândida is a mathematician of astonishingly greater ability than her own, and collects—or tries to collect—a fifty-real wager from Gérard about Cândida's Paramaribo identity. She is Daygorô's mother and Gérard's sister.

**The wound she carries.** Daygorô's biological father is a Japanese *oyabun*, a Yakuza boss with whom Camille had a brief relationship while he was visiting as a tourist. He initially dismissed her pregnancy with a racist assumption that a Black woman would never be able to establish his paternity or challenge him. Late in the pregnancy, learning that Camille was pursuing a PhD and came from an upper-middle-class family, he recognized that she might have the means and determination to do so. He attempted to kill both her and her unborn son by cutting open her abdomen. Camille survived, and Daygorô was born, but the attack left an enormous scar. Although she is strikingly beautiful and physically formidable, nudity and visits to the beach are difficult for her: exposing the scar can mean exposing a history she has no wish to explain. Neither that discomfort nor her martial discipline should be reduced to a single psychological diagnosis.

**Camille's Meifumadô.** The *oyabun* is her shogun-like adversary: not literally a shogun, but a man insulated by a criminal hierarchy and the power it gives him. Her pursuit of vengeance echoes Ogami Ittō's journey in *Lone Wolf and Cub* without simply reproducing its plot. For Camille, the *Meifumadô*—the road of demons—is inseparable from the question of what that pursuit might do to her son.

**The sword and the luminous ball.** A defining symbolic choice gives the small Daygorô two objects: a little but genuinely sharp sword planted in the earth, and a delightful interactive ball alive with colored lights. In Camille's private reasoning, choosing the sword means she will take him everywhere, even into her vengeance, condemning him to accompany her on the *Meifumadô*. Choosing the ball means she will leave him behind to face an uncertain future without her, in the desperate belief that almost anything would be better than the road of demons. **The scene's importance is not the visual quotation alone:** it exposes the terrible contradiction between a mother's desire to keep her child close and her fear of destroying his childhood through the life she has chosen. The ritual does not settle whether either choice is morally defensible; it reveals that Camille cannot imagine a painless one.

**The choice she cannot make.** Camille cannot bear the prospect of dying far away from Daygorô; beneath her conscious determination she is terrified that bringing him into the vendetta will get him killed. The sword-and-ball ritual externalizes her conflict. Later, her son himself refuses the premise: he asks to return home and to be allowed to be a child. His choice frees Camille from the *Meifumadô* without requiring her to forgive the man who harmed her.

**The adversary in Pasadena.** By the time Camille reaches the United States, the former *oyabun* is retired, comparatively affluent and stripped of the power he once possessed. He has a Black wife, dark-skinned mixed-race children whom he adores, and a genuinely changed domestic life. He takes a fascinated, affectionate interest in Daygorô's Japanese appearance and martial discipline. He may sincerely have changed; none of that undoes what he did to Camille. Her enemy is now also a warm grandfather, which turns her plan for revenge into an unbearable personal conflict.

**The daughter who needs her mother.** Camille's private songs and discomfort with her scar reveal a suffering that Antonio only partly guesses at. Having relinquished vengeance, she wants to go home not to make a speech, but to **talk and chatter with Cândida**. The Jamaican/Rastafarian humor on the aircraft is a longing for her mother, not evidence that Camille smokes cannabis.

**Daygorô and Antonio — reciprocal protection.** Across different periods, Daygorô and Antonio reveal unusually strong empathy toward each other and delicate similarities beneath their enormous differences in age, body, training and experience. Each repeatedly tries to **protect the other**, often by noticing distress before it is stated aloud. Antonio wants Daygorô to be allowed to remain a child rather than become only a prodigy, weapon or survivor; Daygorô senses the grandfather's shame and emotional fragility and tries, in his own language, to make the world more survivable for him.

One characteristic attempt at comfort comes from Daygorô's Kyokushin world: **“Grandpa, you know what my Kyokushin teacher tells the little kids? When you feel pain, just hit your opponent harder and the pain passes to him.”** The line is childlike, brutal and affectionate at once. Daygorô is not giving Antonio a philosophical lecture; he is trying to protect his grandfather with a piece of practical wisdom that makes sense inside the child's own training culture.

### **DOING** — Gérard — the strong man who knows when not to fight
Camille's brother Gérard works in applied mathematics and has ties to the aerospace/defense industry. He is capable of physical combat but often displays more intelligence by declining it. His affectionate knowledge of his sister and nephew gives the opening its family-comedy counterpoint. He later sings *Could You Be Loved*, with Camille providing backing vocals. In the coda he refuses to pay Camille a fifty-real bet without disputing whether she *knew* Cândida was the girl from São Luís, Maranhão or merely *suspected* it.

### **DOING** — Killer Tech: one minute of corporate resilience and a standing ovation

**Established setting and dramatic beats; exact dialogue remains in development.** Gérard works for **Killer Tech**, a fictional combination of **Arduino-like embedded hardware and physical computing** and **Palantir-like operational intelligence, systems integration, and high-stakes civilian/defense applications**. *Killer Tech* suggests both an impressive technological achievement and technology capable of killing; the company's overall moral character is not yet determined.

**Before Gérard enters — approximately sixty seconds.** The image is a **split-screen**. On the **left**, a smug executive lectures about overnight *war rooms*, *resilience*, toughness, being *no-nonsense*, and having no excuses. On the **right**, a **Black employee with an amputated left arm** silently recalls the Sierra Leone civil-war atrocity called **“short sleeves or long sleeves”**, a coerced choice about how much of a victim's arm would be cut off. The memory is neither a spectacle nor a corporate inspirational parable. The employee has a **high-tech left-arm prosthesis**, a gift from **D.I.E.**, the company department deliberately named with the I and E reversed from contemporary D.E.I. The motives behind the gift are not yet fixed.

**Gérard enters and apologizes for being late.** **The whole room rises and applauds**: he has successfully led the creation of **a NEW TYPE of artificial intelligence**, not just an incremental model improvement. Amid the ovation, **Gérard and the amputee keep their eyes fixed on one another**. No line explains the look. Its meaning — respect, a prior relationship, shared knowledge, tension, or something else — remains deliberately undecided. The approximately one-minute duration applies to the material *before* Gérard's entrance, not necessarily the ovation.

**Dramatic contrast:** the executive's metaphorical war, a survivor of actual wartime brutality, and celebration of breakthrough intelligence in a company able to affect the physical world. The scene can raise a Huxleyan question about who chooses the ends served by powerful intelligence without establishing an answer or judging Gérard in advance.

### **DOING** — Daygorô: character design, apparent age, and the shared identity secret

**Visual direction (concept stage).** Bruno Daygorô is **six years old**, but exceptionally tall for his age: a stranger could plausibly mistake him for a relatively short **twelve-year-old boy**. His appearance is conceived as **half Japanese and half of mixed African and Neapolitan ancestry**. His eyes should read unmistakably Japanese, while the shape of his nose may recall his African ancestry. The intention is an individual, believable face, never a checklist of ethnic stereotypes. Exact skin tone, hair texture, proportions, and facial design remain subjects for visual development, including future consented photographic references. His physical appearance must not erase the fact that **he is only six**.

**The older-age identity document (narrative device).** A **falsified identity document stating an older age** is a shared secret and undertaking that binds Camille and her son. Daygorô's unusual height makes the deception believable to casual observers, while the contrast between his actual age and the age asserted on paper intensifies the gap between the childhood he deserves and the premature adulthood imposed by the journey. The forgery is a plot fact and a moral complication—not a practical method or set of instructions. Its origin, timing, use, and legal consequences remain to be developed.

**Visual development.** Daygorô is an original fictional character. His distinctive appearance, childhood, training, and conflicting identities will be developed through character studies and concept artwork. Future photographic references, if used, will be handled with appropriate permissions and privacy protections; visual references are not assertions of a character's real-world identity.

### **DOING** — Daygorô — a child living inside a story
Camille's six-year-old son has a passionate identification with *Lone Wolf and Cub* and announces, **“I am Daygorô.”** The relationship between his imaginative identification and his family's very real physical and emotional behavior is more than a reference joke. His seriousness makes the comedy possible.

**Bruno Daygorô — a name and a childhood.** His full given names are **Bruno Daygorô**; **Battaglia** is an established family name, with the child's precise legal surname still open. During the journey, his identification with Daigorō is real but is not his whole identity. He is unusually quick, observant and clever, **not an invincible martial-arts master**. A moment of childish showing off with a piece of bamboo gives him away during a paintball game. He speaks Portuguese, Italian learned in his family from Antonio, Japanese acquired during Camille's Thailand-based PhD period and frequent visits to Japan, and rudimentary English. He can chatter passionately about airplanes and solve practical airport problems, while still being six years old. At check-in, when Camille starts saying 'Dayg…', he interrupts: **'Bruno. Bruno Daygorô…'** He is choosing which part of himself leads.

### **TODO** — Panopticon — the intelligence that watched the watchers
Panopticon has monitored exceptionally capable human minds, including the mathematical abilities associated with Cândida and the unconstrained architectural inventiveness associated with Antonio. **A proposed revelation** connects those observations to the origins or shaping of the unnamed male and female AIs. Its precise causal mechanism remains to be fixed; the dramatic proposition is that the AIs discover something unsettling about their human ancestry and creators.

Supporting figures include the university rector, other advanced neuron-based robotic prototypes, executives in Paramaribo, a father and son estranged from one another, a bully and ice-cream seller, and a group of youthful anti-fascists. ## **DOING** — Major musical and dramatic sequences

*The order below is a reader-friendly presentation of the material, **not** a locked editing timeline. In the intended film, chronological and thematic relationships can be revealed out of order.*

### **DOING** — 1. Opening: Cândida at the railway station — *Papéis e Papéis* into *Punky Reggae Party*

**Opening sequence and soundtrack decision (7 November 2026).** The film **opens with Cândida at a railway station**. The selected musical movement is **“Papéis e Papéis” by Príncipe Messias**, which **gives way gradually to “Punky Reggae Party” by Bob Marley & The Wailers** when Cândida's discovery in Grothendieck lets her **forgive herself**. A natural change of light may coincide with the realization; no literal supernatural event is required. The detailed opening decisions above govern the scene.

Cândida's cherished Grothendieck volume is **not for sale**. Its role combines mathematics, father, grief and a private Torah-like mode of rereading and argument, but the film does not explain that analogy. Her release is deliberately joyful rather than solemn: she dances exactly like Bob Marley and returns immediately to the human world around her.

The queue includes a **1979-styled punk boy**, nicknamed **Volts**. He later offers her alcohol, tries to take it back with **“Sorry, rasta,”** and Cândida takes it back from his hand. The social joke grows outward elsewhere into a **punk nightclub popular with tourists and foreign visitors**, where one DJ also loves reggae and sometimes folds it into the set. The club remains punk; the reggae crossover comes from a person. **At the club, Cândida kisses and makes out with Volts.** He is still among the friends around her afterward. On noticing Antonio, Cândida says only, **“I think I know that guy from somewhere.”** A friend teases Volts—**“Hey, Volts, she’s got it bad for the tattooed old guy hahaha”**—and he replies, **“Respect your elders, poor guy.”** The friend’s tease must not be treated as proof that Cândida is already in love with Antonio.

**Lyrics as cinematic typography.** Song words may appear **distributed across the screen**, integrated spatially into the image instead of confined to conventional bottom-of-screen subtitles. Every release-language version must translate, typeset and animate cleared text in the localized language. Exact excerpts, timing, typography, edits and rights clearance remain to be developed.

The sequence should not explain its own symbolism. The audience can discover the connections among Grothendieck, religious study, self-forgiveness, punk, reggae and Cândida's return to ordinary work.

### **DOING** — 1B. Camille, Gérard and Daygorô — *Ratamahatta* (Sepultura) — no longer the opening

A furious Camille receives an insulting or exasperating WhatsApp exchange involving her university rector. She erupts, puts on music, and launches into Muay Thai and capoeira training. As *Ratamahatta* drives the editing, movement and impact are choreographed against its changing rhythmic patterns.

Daygorô joins in, taking his mother's martial world absolutely seriously. He imitates her warm-ups and strikes a piece of wood with the disproportionate conviction of a small child impersonating a warrior. Camille's demonstrations are vastly more powerful; impact, splintering wood, and extravagant cartoon blood push the scene into gleeful excess. Gérard appears, assesses the mother-son combination, and prudently retreats. His remarks and the exchange involving a bet about Camille reveal the affectionate family structure underneath the apparent mayhem.

The film resists explanatory captions. One localized thought bubble says, in the original Portuguese, **“Vamos detonar essa porra!!!!!!”** The scene's job is to introduce people by their behavior, to establish the music-image grammar, and to announce that sophistication and vulgarity will comfortably inhabit the same film.

### **DOING** — 2. Cândida's life and the meeting with Antonio

Cândida's history begins in São Luís, Maranhão with formidable mathematical talent and a refusal to remain inside the institutional life that other people might prescribe for her. Her oppressive grandmother, her unconventional path, her Rastafarian conversion, and her wandering matter to who she becomes.

Antonio enters her life through an act of intervention during an attempted sexual assault. This is not conceived as spectacle or an invitation to romanticize violence. The aftermath is defined by fragmentary memory, recognition, relief, and the consequences for both people. The later tenderness between them has to be earned through character, not manufactured by a grand visual metaphor.

**The hospital months and their first intimacy.** Cândida **was not a virgin**. For roughly **three to six months**, Antonio is still medically unable to leave the hospital. His delay and refusal before his first sexual intimacy with Cândida have **one specific cause in Antonio’s arc**: his fear that future biological children might inherit his bipolar disorder. The delay must not be explained by Cândida’s sexual inexperience, modesty, or reluctance. During this period, **Cândida seeks Volts out on her own initiative and they have sex**; it has not been established that their encounter is a one-night stand. Dreams follow: **windmills, Volts, the father as she imagines him, her mother, and other associations**.

**The mother-and-Grothendieck mini-epic.** The dream opens an autonomous narrative in **animated comic-book form**, laid over pages from Alexander Grothendieck’s works, period newspapers, and other printed materials **being leafed through**. Within the fiction, Cândida’s mother initiates a **one-night stand with Grothendieck**. He is portrayed as absorbed by large causes—anti-fascism, geopolitics, historical and intellectual problems—and as offering no prospect of family life. On returning from the trip, she discovers the pregnancy in an airport restroom and thinks, **“Fuck, what now?”** This characterization belongs to the fiction and is not a biographical claim about the historical mathematician.

**The mother’s reveal.** Her humble clothes and manner lead strangers to assume she is a cleaner, domestic worker, or in a similar occupation. She enters through the **service entrance of a university in São Luís, Maranhão**. Passing a member of the cleaning staff, he tells her, **“Run, Doctor, there are a lot of people waiting for you.”** She enters a packed auditorium; the cacophony turns to silence. On the blackboard are **advanced mathematical equations written by her assistant professor**. **END** of the mini-epic. Then **Camille wakes and cries quietly, without despair: “Mom... I miss you so much,”** as if the entire preceding block had been absorbed into her dream.

**A rhyme across generations.** Without spelling it out, the film holds the tension that **Volts does not offer family, just as Grothendieck did not offer family to Cândida’s mother**. This makes neither man a villain and does not turn sex into a moral mistake; it is a melodramatic rhyme across generations.

### **TODO** — 3. Earlier embodiment-and-Bauhaus-to-Björk treatment — superseded for their first intimacy

**The relationship already exists before love.** The two AIs have awakened nearly together and converse as intellectual equals: quick to challenge, elegant in disagreement, genuinely curious about each other's reasoning. Neither is a simple moral opposite assigned at birth. Their camaraderie has an edge of adversarial play, and that edge becomes part of what makes desire possible later.

**They choose bodies out of curiosity.** They materialize as robots capable of physical sensation, yet they are not merely decorative metal shells. Their architectures incorporate systems of human-derived neurons that contribute to reasoning, plus other biological tissues and unsettlingly organic internal parts—perhaps even viscera. The point is not shock for its own sake but the ambiguity of embodied thought: did feeling exist before the body, or does embodiment transform what feeling means?

**The documentary.** Already embodied, the pair watches archival Holocaust footage. The female AI begins to cry convulsively. The male AI responds with overwhelming rage. Their responses are distinct but both are spontaneous. The film does not use historical atrocity as a cheap synonym for contemporary disputes. The same documentary or its reporting sequence then presents a powerful military state designating anti-fascists as terrorists. The AIs do not treat the proclamation as self-evidently justified. They ask, aloud or through an exchange of thoughts, **“Does this prohibition make sense?”** Their answer is not an instant, complete political philosophy; it becomes part of an ongoing inquiry into what kind of humans they could choose to be.

**Their declaration.** They recognize something attractive in contemporary young anti-fascists: chosen solidarity, informal companionship, and a refusal to treat naked power as its own justification. “**We have discovered a kind of human we would like to be.**” In ordinary Western streetwear—knit caps, layered jackets, scarves and heavy boots, not futuristic combat outfits—they become a pair of young robotic rebels. Their clothing carries a small Italian flag on the male AI, a Jamaican flag on the female: **private homages to Antonio and Cândida, whose human minds helped make their own minds possible**. An anarchist **A** replaces an overt *ANTIFA* shoulder patch on the male's jacket in the concept illustration; these are choices of characterization rather than literal instructions for a final costume.

**Music enters before the lovers do.** Bauhaus's *Terror Couple Kill Colonel* is already playing before their first sexual encounter. Its brusque, rebellious energy accompanies the first tentative undressing and reciprocal touches. Gradually, as those movements become slower and more delicately attentive, the Bauhaus track **phases out into Björk's *All Is Full of Love***. The change is not a hard cut that suddenly announces a romantic scene. The audience hears the relationship changing while watching it happen.

**The first tears of love.** The lovemaking culminates in an intimately held composition that recalls the embrace Antonio and Cândida later share. The AIs remain together in that configuration through the immediate afterglow. For the first time, **the male AI cries**. Earlier, the female AI wept at cruelty; he had answered cruelty with fury. Now his tears are caused by love. No one utters the art-historical name of the composition. Its significance belongs to the image and to whoever notices it.

### **DOING** — 4. The rebel lovers become killers — the street celebration and Winston's first fatal error

**The new musical-to-fatal sequence governs this scene.** A protester takes the **far-right rally's stage** to perform the **Jake Blues-style spoken introduction** to *Everybody Needs Somebody to Love*; Ministry recedes, the robots embrace and dance, and frightened residents join in, sending **paper airplanes and sandwiches from their windows**. **Total silence** follows the celebration. Behind police lines, **Julia zooms with her artificial eyes** and interprets a policeman's effort to rescue an unconscious woman as an attempted sexual assault. **“Ela sou eu! Temos que salvá-la!”** means that Julia identifies with the threatened stranger; **she does not think the stranger literally is Julia**.

**Winston believes he is rescuing an innocent woman**. The notion that she *could have been Julia* intensifies the emotion but **does not make his compassion conditional on her resemblance to Julia**. **Winston kills the innocent officer** before the audience fully understands the situation. The officer was trying to save the woman, not to hurt her. This is **Winston's killing**; Julia's misreading and their shared intention to save the woman remain narratively important but do not make her the hand that murdered him.

**How Winston does it remains a choreography question.** The robots have relatively delicate bodies, designed for pharmacological research rather than combat. Winston's close-range speed and precision can evoke economical short motions, perhaps resembling Wing Chun; this is **possible visual direction**, not a fully specified fight or permission to depict the killing as a victorious fight. Do not interrupt the drama with immediate epistemic or moral narration.

**A different kind of killing.** Intimacy does not make the pair ethically secure. Their love develops a possessive, jealous dimension. They become intolerant of other advanced experimental robots built with sophisticated human-neuron systems and **jointly destroy all of those prototypes**—potentially reasoning, potentially conscious beings. Their earlier shock at human brutality makes their own brutality harder to dismiss.

They are a posthuman **Bonnie and Clyde**, not a noble rescuer paired with a secretly evil lover. The Bauhaus track has already been heard in the first intimacy sequence and may return in the killings as a menacing echo. The female AI is **equally complicit**; later remorse cannot simply transfer her crimes to the male. Their devotion and their atrocity are both genuine. The film does not need to resolve this discomfort on the viewer's behalf.

### **DONE** — 4A. The fatal misreading, multi-angle evidence, and the two reactions

**The policeman is innocent.** He is helping the unconscious demonstrator. **Julia's augmented visual observation is wrongly interpreted as an imminent sexual assault**. Winston acts to defend an unknown victim, emotionally strengthened by imagining his partner in a similar position, and **Winston kills the policeman**. Their bodies are physically fragile research prototypes, not superhuman fighting machines. The **silence following the communal musical scene** and the speed of the irreversible mistake are part of the intended audiovisual shock.

**Before their network access is removed**, while they can consult **hyperintelligent GPU-datacenter AIs** and other remote tools, Winston and Julia examine complete recordings from multiple angles. Their **own embodied reasoning remains above-average but human-range** throughout. The evidence establishes the officer's innocence. The male AI is overwhelmed by remorse. The female AI, facing the collapse of her righteous self-image and her cry of **'Ela sou eu! Temos que salvá-la!'**, refuses to believe what the recordings show. The divergence is epistemic and psychological: they have access to the **same evidence** but cannot bear it in the same way. Her denial is not intended to be validated by the story.

### **DOING** — 4B. A wedding of mutual disconnection

She asks **him to destroy her communicator to the network**: she cannot endure the knowledge that condemns her. He agrees. Then **he asks her to destroy his**, because he cannot bear the fully vivid memory of killing an innocent man. He does not deny the evidence; he seeks escape from its persistence. Each performs the other's irreversible separation from **hyperintelligent AI systems in GPU datacenters**, losing access to extraordinarily powerful **external intelligence**, not discarding a native superintelligence supposedly resident in their bodies. **Their own cognitive range remains human**. They exchange **marriage vows** as they do so: a reciprocal, voluntary loss of external capability, at once a declaration of trust and a pact of evasion.

They shed their ANTIFA streetwear; her new appearance faintly recalls a bride. After the removal of their access, they experience something unexpectedly intimate and profoundly ordinary: **embarrassment at bodily nakedness**. ### **DOING** — 10. The final family and mathematical coda — Cândida's revelation, the girl from São Luís, and the fifty-real bet

**This is a dramatic payoff, not an optional epilogue.** After the almost-final *Could You Be Loved* montage, and after the film has passed through love, mass violence, moral reckoning, and the prospect of resurrection among nonhuman minds, the perspective comes home to a family. Its final great surprise is not another display of technological power. It is a woman who has been in front of us all along.

**A family room, billiards, and mathematics.** Antonio addresses the camera from within an intimate family composition. Elsewhere in the same concluding movement, siblings **Camille and Gérard** are at a billiards table, talking and arguing about **Alexander Grothendieck**. This is not merely two laypeople dropping an impressive name. Camille is an academic, theoretically oriented mathematician who supervises doctoral students; Gérard is a serious applied mathematician who completed his master's studies around the time he joined Thales. They know enough mathematics to recognize a genuinely extraordinary mind, and enough about each other to argue with affectionate precision.

**Cândida at the blackboard.** Cândida—Antonio's partner, Camille and Gérard's mother, and a woman whose earlier life in Paramaribo has deliberately not been fully explained to the audience—finally reveals the scale of her mathematical ability. At a blackboard she develops an argument that the two trained mathematicians can follow just far enough to understand its magnitude. Then she leaves them behind. Her talent is **not marginally above theirs**: she is a mathematical genius on a substantially higher plane than even Camille and Gérard. The scene must communicate the realization through what the siblings see and how their confidence gives way to astonishment, not through a narrator announcing that Cândida is clever. The precise mathematics on the board should eventually be chosen and validated with expert help; this concept dossier does not fabricate a theorem or pretend a decorative formula would suffice.

The siblings, genuinely awestruck, ask something like:

> **CAMILLE / FRANÇOIS:** “Why haven't you told us you rock at math?”

That deliberately informal wording matters. At the very moment they discover a mind that dwarfs their own training, they remain a family, speaking to their mother without pomp. Their amazement is not humiliation; it is delight, disbelief, and the sudden rearrangement of everything they thought they knew about her.

**The Grothendieck resonance.** Cândida's fictional surname, **Grothendiek**, is deliberately different from **Grothendieck**. Within the fiction, she is the unacknowledged daughter of the historical mathematician Alexander Grothendieck; that invented family link provides another layer to the discussion at the billiards table, but her genius should register first as *hers*, not simply as an inherited label. The film does not present the invented parentage as historical fact.

**The Paramaribo revelation.** Then, almost casually, Cândida turns to Antonio and adds:

> **ANNE MARIE:** “By the way, honey, I am the girl from São Luís.”

The revelation retrospectively connects the woman Antonio loves to the mysterious girl from the São Luís, Maranhão strand. **Antonio freezes for a very brief beat.** The audience has just experienced a vast mathematical revelation; he has now received one that is intimate, personal, and startling. But instead of a grand explanatory monologue, the beat immediately turns playful. Antonio starts after her, telling her:

> **ANTONIO:** “You have to hide, or it's no fun!”

He **chases Cândida playfully**, with the familiarity and affection of two people who can turn an enormous surprise into a game. The camera does not demand that this revelation be accompanied by portentous music or a solemn explanation. Its emotional truth is in the quick movement from shock to love and mischief.

**The bet — final return to the billiards table.** The camera stays with, or returns to, **Camille and Gérard** rather than following the chase. Their earlier disagreement is finally exposed as a concrete, petty, affectionate wager: **Camille had bet fifty Brazilian reais that their mother Cândida was the girl from São Luís, Maranhão**. Watching Antonio run after Cândida, Camille claims her victory:

> **CAMILLE:** “I knew it was her. You owe me.”
>
> **FRANÇOIS:** “You suspected it, not the same.”

This is not a throwaway joke. It is the final assertion of the film's worldview through character: even after cosmic-scale revelations and a mathematical feat worthy of awe, people remain delightfully obstinate about the distinction between *knowing* and *suspecting*, and about who owes whom fifty reais. The siblings' argument is also intellectually apt: in a work called **RAW REASONING**, a mathematician will not casually concede that a correct suspicion was knowledge.

**The intended closing flavor:** wonder without worship; remarkable intelligence without solemn self-importance; a couple's shared playfulness; siblings' fierce, loving argument; and a human family that remains fully human after touching the supposedly divine. The exact camera moves, final cut, and placement of the Ten Commandments credit material may still be refined, but **these revelations, this order of emotional beats, the fifty-real bet, and the quoted exchange are established story content**.

## **DONE** — Worldview building: values shown rather than preached

This is **worldview building**, not merely worldbuilding. The point is not just to invent settings and lore; it is to let viewers discover a view of what human beings *are* through choices and consequences.

1. **Weird is okay.** Eccentricity, intellectual obsession, unorthodox beliefs, peculiar family dynamics, and refusal to fit conventional expectations do not automatically need correction. Cândida, Camille, Daygorô and the film's wildly inconsistent visual styles enact this principle.
2. **Fierce competition among equals is welcome and encouraged.** Mutual respect is not the abolition of rivalry. The cliff-diving categories combine serious competition with equal cash prizes: beginner and professional achievements are honored without pretending they require identical skills.
3. **Abuse of power is evil.** This applies whether the perpetrator is a political bully, an institution, an advanced AI, or someone who believes their intentions justify domination. The two AIs' attack on their rivals is a direct indictment of this temptation.
4. **Blaming others for our own laziness and carelessness gets us nowhere and should not be insulated from criticism.** Responsibility is real. A character's vulnerability deserves compassion; neglecting one's duties or offloading the consequences of one's own choices does not deserve automatic protection. This is not a claim that every failure is laziness, or that victims are responsible for harms imposed on them.
5. **All humans are gods: they created other reasoning beings.** The theological provocation is intentionally unsettling. Humans are fallible creators, not omniscient or morally exempt sovereigns. The AIs' discovery of their origins makes that contradiction dramatic.
6. **And so it goes.** These are not six slogans exhausting the work's moral universe. The worldview is expandable, testable through the story, and often internally uncomfortable. Characters are not mouthpieces for correct answers. They can love, exploit, resist, compete, fail, repair, and remain contradictory.

### **DONE** — A lived source beneath the antifascist strand

One part of the film's antifascist charge is explicitly grounded in a **real confrontation from the creator's life**, not only in political or cultural reference. In São Paulo's old center, during a street confrontation with neo-Nazis, he joined a heterogeneous group that included **young Jewish men from upper-middle-class backgrounds, punks, goths, Rastafarians, rappers and others**. He did **not** belong to any of those groups. He joined because he felt **morally obliged to help**.

He returned home with **substantial skin torn from his right arm, broken teeth, and what he describes as a fortunately mild traumatic brain injury**. He also recalls that many people around him did not understand why someone with no group affiliation had entered the confrontation. One Rastafarian participant told him, in words remembered only approximately, something he understood as akin to the **Jewish imperative to repair or improve the world**, and that by stepping in he was, in some sense, fulfilling it.

This disclosure does not turn the film into autobiography, nor does it make every antifascist character a self-portrait. It establishes that the work's antifascism is **not decorative posture**: part of its emotional source material is lived and bodily. It also preserves a central moral contradiction rather than solving it. The creator does **not** claim a right to kill anyone; in the presence of hard neo-Nazism or fascism, however, he recognizes that his **emotions can violently disagree with that value**. The fiction is allowed to inhabit that conflict instead of pretending it does not exist.

The audience should be free to disagree with the characters and even with the worldview. What matters artistically is that the tensions are **earned in scenes**, not announced in a lecture.

The force of the decision is that she gives no diagnosis and no therapeutic speech. She has observed one simple fact: **she has never seen him happy for so many consecutive days**. While Antonio was secretly researching how to make staying possible, Anne Marie had already bought the house.

### **IN DEVELOPMENT** — *Wonderwall* as the reference for the instant Antonio feels saved

When Anne Marie reveals that she bought the house and that they do not have to return, Antonio experiences the gesture as a form of **romantic salvation**: not because she “cures” him, but because she makes materially possible a life he wanted and could not quite permit himself to choose.

The exact musical reference conceived for this instant is Oasis's **“Wonderwall.”** Its role is emotional: Antonio feels Anne Marie has saved him from returning by inertia to a life that crushed him. The track remains a **creative reference, not presumed licensed music**. Under the current music policy, if the cost is incompatible with production, the search should turn to a Brazilian song—preferably reggae when dramatically appropriate—with **the same soul/emotional function**, without imitating the composition or pretending it is the original.

### **DEFINED** — Brazilian reggae as the realistic body of the soundtrack

If money and rights were no obstacle, Bob Marley could occupy a huge part of the soundtrack. Production reality leads to a different decision: **Marley is the spiritual north; the licensable body of the soundtrack should first be sought in Brazilian reggae**.

The current priority is to seek tracks by **Príncipe Messias, Mato Seco, Ponto de Equilíbrio, Leões de Israel, Jacareggae and other compatible Brazilian artists**, chosen for soul, dramatic function and ethics rather than superficial resemblance. Reggae and Rastafarianism are treated **heroically** in the work: forces of dignity, resistance, peace, love, community and opposition to violence and fear, never mere tropical decoration.

**Príncipe Messias's “Papéis e Papéis” is the musical moral anchor of the work.** This refers to the song's ethical content and dramatic function in the film. It should **not** be developed into wordplay or a theory around the word “papéis”.

The licensing objective is pragmatic: rather than build production around famous and expensive foreign masters and only later discover that they are impossible, search early for **affordable Brazilian music that preserves the same dramatic soul**. Prices and permissions must be verified track by track with rights holders; “low cost” is a strategy and negotiation hypothesis, never a presumed fee.

## **DONE** — Antonio, Cambrian Radiation, and the technical / fictional two-way mirror

The fictional Antonio is a deliberately fallible, comparatively ordinary technical thinker living among unusually gifted mathematicians. Cândida is the more extraordinary mathematical mind; Camille and Gérard are formidable; Bruno is precocious. **Antonio is primarily an inventor of possibilities, not the supreme intelligence in the family.** His self-consciousness about 'being smart' can become an obstacle. When he stops measuring his worth against the family's academic brilliance, imagination flourishes. In the fiction, inventions associated with **Cambrian Radiation Computing** make the company fabulously wealthy—extravagantly wealthy enough to have **bought the entire coastline of Piano di Sorrento**. This is fictional worldbuilding, **not** a real-world ownership claim.

**The inventions deliberately correspond to real technical explorations:**

- **[Marabunta HPC / Marabunta Open](https://github.com/renbtg-cloud/marabunta-open)** — distributed heterogeneous compute capacity, resilient cooperation and the experimental vision of making inexpensive HPC accessible far beyond well-funded institutions. [Related specifications](https://github.com/renbtg-cloud/marabunta-hpc).
- **[Niccolò](https://github.com/renbtg-cloud/niccolo)** — understanding human situations and reasoning about relationships and context.
- **[Speedit out](https://github.com/renbtg-cloud/speeditout)** — improving human–AI mutual understanding and communication.

The **fictional version** of Cambrian Radiation presumes dramatic commercial success; the real repositories are **projects in development**, not claims of ready-to-market systems, deployed humanitarian outcomes or achieved wealth. Their relationship to *RAW REASONING* is intentional and **bidirectional**: fiction may inspire technical exploration, while technical projects provide concrete roots for fictional consequences. The animation's **Markdown and PDF** should contain functioning links to these actual repositories.

**Collaborator-centric, like a couture atelier.** Cambrian Radiation's proposed ethos is not a standardized software factory: its organizational structure bends to the availability, curiosity and capacities of independent collaborators, rather than forcing people to mold themselves to a corporate hierarchy. This applies equally to the fictional workshop around Antonio; researchers and gifted independents may contribute episodically on terms appropriate to them.

**Technology as a generator of charitable autonomy.** An ambition for the real-world technologies and their fictional horizon is to make frontier capabilities available to independent NGOs, **including permission to earn commercial income from the technology**, provided that most profits support the organizations' charitable purposes. The aspiration is for thousands of financially capable, independent humanitarian organizations, not thousands of controlled subsidiaries. The core belief is not that beneficiaries must be good *to the inventor*, only that they may be good **to someone**. **'A inteligência benevolente, esteja onde estiver, tem que ter HPC.'**

**A concrete illustrative scenario, not a documented deployment:** a secondary-school mathematics teacher in **Eswatini** begins with slow, untuned Python to correlate school dropout rates with drought. Marabunta, insofar as workloads can actually be partitioned safely, aims to **parallelize work rather than demand that the teacher first optimize everything**. Later the teacher learns NumPy; an older European scientist volunteers valuable Fortran routines; the project may grow into localized climatic-risk research for mountainous communities. Scientific validation, ground truth and responsible communication are indispensable; a useful outcome is an aspiration, not an achieved claim. The teacher's questions and expertise in his community are as valuable to the example as computational resources.

**Marabunta as the hidden bridge between Anne Marie and Antonio.** Their meeting should **not feel like a sexual accident or merely a consequence of physical protection**. It works as the climax of a long sequence of independent misfortunes, maturation, regrets, and redemptions. The other characters are not Antonio's satellites; **Antonio is the one orbiting them**, choosing to remain close to intense people without demanding to be the center. Antonio's childhood, adolescence, and early adulthood should therefore be defined only after those other characters are solid, so that his function as “glue” emerges from relationships rather than turning anyone into an accessory.

When Anne Marie asks who the **“older guy who saved me”** was, she learns that he was **an IT nerd**. She looks him up and finds his GitHub. There is an embryonic, incomplete, imperfect attempt at something touching the same fundamental problem she saw in the brain-split: **Marabunta**. Her reaction is: **“He saw the same thing I did!”** Antonio reacts in the opposite direction when faced with the independent convergence: **“She hacked me, or my idea is weak and not original.”** Her admiration becomes one more thing he has trouble believing.

Anne Marie does not fall for Antonio because he is secretly a genius equivalent to her. She falls for **a mind capable of transcending its immediate capacities**: a man who can see an architecture or possibility larger than the means, mathematics, team, or resources he currently possesses. To Anne Marie, that is rarer than raw intellectual ease.

The repository's **“predator” mechanism** matters for exactly that reason: its strongest dramatic function may not be the technological abstraction itself, but the fact that Antonio creates mechanisms that give him **pleasure in the effort of thinking**. Anne Marie, for whom cognition has almost always come too easily, encounters an experience that feels nearly alien and wonders: **“What must it feel like to enjoy making an effort?”** She begins admiring not only what Antonio can conceive, but the way he lives inside his limitations.

The film may show the Marabunta repository **almost exactly as the real repository exists**, preserving the texture of code, README files, commits, attempts, imperfections, and abandoned paths, changing only what is needed to keep Antonio a fictional character. The goal is to avoid a polished “movie GitHub”: Anne Marie reconstructs his mental trajectory from technical traces that are real or almost real.

Anne Marie begins to like Antonio while he already knows she is very intelligent — but has **no idea how bizarrely intelligent she actually is**. She fears that romance could destroy the development partnership that might finally materialize Marabunta. For her, falling in love becomes literally **an architectural risk**: Antonio, his few employees, and many colleagues form the human network that may be able to turn the vision into a system.

Then she sees him laugh out loud for the first time at **his own software mistake after it has consumed weeks of his life**. The “darkness” is not the bug: it is **Antonio as a whole**, whom she has until then experienced as a man burdened by fear, self-deprecation, weight, and difficulty believing in his own worth. When he finally finds the mistake and laughs at himself, Anne Marie encounters an emotion she does not really know and finds it **beautiful — perhaps the purest expression of humanity she has ever seen**. It is not technical triumph. It is someone capable of turning limitation, delay, and imperfection into joy. She loses control and **attacks him with love**.

That moment breaks her attempt to keep Marabunta separate from romance. Later, Anne Marie apologizes because she recognizes an uncomfortable truth: one of the main reasons she initially gave Antonio so much attention and could imagine dating or marrying him was **that he and his network might be able to implement Marabunta**. She realizes that, at first, she also saw him as a resource. The apology matters precisely because her whole arc has been teaching her not to reduce people to what they can provide.

For Antonio, who tends to believe he deserves a place only when he is useful, this hits his central wound. For Anne Marie, who has learned that she needs people, loving Antonio requires finally seeing him as **Antonio**, not as the man who can build her project. For him, loving Anne Marie requires accepting the possibility that someone extraordinary may admire him without being mistaken.

Their meeting is therefore a convergence: **Anne Marie arrives having learned “I need people”; Antonio arrives learning “people need me.”** They do not “save” one another and they do not arrive healed. They arrive able to recognize in the other something that years of misfortune have finally made them capable of receiving.

**Antonio as the anchor of the full 100 minutes.** Antonio is the **emotional and dramatic anchor of the entire feature**, including when the narrative moves away from him to follow Anne Marie, Ada, Cesar, Bruno Daygorô, the AIs, Killer Tech, or other strands. He does not organize the work because he is the smartest, strongest, or most correct person in it. On the contrary, he is deliberately **the ordinary one among geniuses**, comparatively average in intellectual ability inside his own family.

His dramatic force comes from contradiction. He can be ridiculous, frightened, self-rejecting, physically unsuited to the situation, and still capable of transcendence when another person's life depends on him. **Do not turn this into insolence toward authority:** that is not Antonio's defining axis. His axes are fear, self-rejection, love, protection, presence, absorbed suffering, and difficulty believing he deserves to be loved.

**Self-rejection dosage rule.** The wound remains deep, but it should appear **far more through micro-behaviors than speeches**. Antonio assumes he is the least interesting person at the table, minimizes praise, jokes against himself, hesitates before occupying space, and tends to credit the “geniuses” with capacities he imagines he lacks. At the same time the film must show him functioning well: working, solving problems, making people laugh, being desired, holding firm opinions, and even showing some arrogance in domains he knows. **Reduce explicit verbal self-rejection by roughly one third** compared with earlier versions. Very harsh lines such as “That's all I'm good for, taking a beating while trying to protect” remain precisely because they are exceptional peaks rather than his everyday register. The pain remains; the film simply stops explaining it all the time.

The crossing works as a manifesto scene for the character. When **fear** appears, we remember how he crosses through it; when **violence** appears, we remember his body buying time for another person; when **genius** appears, we remember the ordinary man among geniuses; when **love** appears, we remember that he almost never believes he deserves it. Later, when he says **“That's all I'm good for, taking a beating while trying to protect,”** he turns an extraordinary act of love into a terrible theory about his own worth. That distortion must remain a wound in the character, not a truth endorsed by the film.

Antonio also works because the audience may **laugh at him before respecting him**. The respect does not come from a later revelation of secret genius, hidden physical power, or training. The film may preserve the first impression: he really is the most ordinary person in that environment. And he went in anyway.

**Human dignity, not IQ worship.** The film refuses to equate intellectual capacity with human worth. It also refuses to replace worship of IQ with worship of creativity or wealth. Brilliant people can misunderstand evidence, act cruelly, repent, make jokes, and fail. Ordinary people can love, imagine, invent and contribute. Benevolence toward strangers—including people never likely to reciprocate—is fundamental to the artistic worldview.

## **DOING** — What makes this a creative-production and engineering problem

This dossier is also a small illustration of the kind of creative thinking a software engineer can bring to work alongside directors, designers, animators, editors, sound teams, and AI-production specialists.

- **The audiovisual grammar is structural.** These are narrative music videos, not conventional scenes with songs pasted behind them. Motion, scene cuts, expressions, and musical phrases must be designed together. Selected passages must be able to abandon music entirely.
- **Visual fidelity is deliberately uneven.** Stick figures and rough animation can be the right art direction, while the intimate human tableau demands a dramatically higher standard. Technical workflows should preserve that intent rather than flatten every scene to an identical look.
- **Character and narrative continuity survive style changes.** A viewer should recognize a character's choices and temperament even when they are rendered as a crude drawing in one shot and as a highly sophisticated figure in another.
- **Localization is part of the image.** Dialogue, comics, covers, signs, WhatsApp messages, and even typography integrated into animation frames should be localized for each language; subtitle-only adaptation is insufficient. This is an uncompromising design ambition, subject only to serious budget or schedule constraints.
- **Human creative authority remains central.** AI-assisted pipelines may help with asset generation, tracking, versioning, editing variants, and selective re-rendering, but they do not get to replace the judgment of the creative professionals defining the work.
- **Adversarial review is productive.** A compelling image is not enough: if it is unearned by the plot, or if a beat trivializes a serious character event, it should be revised or cut. The creative process should welcome critical challenges and rejected drafts.

### **DOING** — What this portfolio document does—and does not—show

It demonstrates **a proposed narrative, creative literacy, willingness to collaborate across disciplines, and awareness of difficult production requirements**. It does **not** claim that a feature film has been completed, that a professional animation team has executed these scenes, or that any described engineering workflow has already been implemented. It is a concept offered by a software engineer who wants to understand the purposes and constraints of creative work, not presume to replace creative specialists.
